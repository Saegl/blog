1A guessing game

I am thinking of a whole number between 1 and 16. You may ask me questions, but only ones I can answer with "yes" or "no". How many questions do you need to be certain of my number?

If you have ever written a binary search you already know the answer. Ask "is it bigger than 8?", and whatever I say, half of the candidates are gone. Eight are left, then four, then two, then one. Four questions, every time, no matter which number I picked.

Now change the game slightly. I live in a mountain town, and each evening you try to find out what tomorrow's weather report says: sunny, cloudy, rain or snow. Four possibilities, so by the same halving argument two questions are always enough. But you also know something about this town: it is sunny on half of all days. Does that knowledge let you get away with fewer questions?

It does, and not by a trick. If your first question is "is it sunny?", then on half of the days you are done after a single question. On the other days you need more than two, but those days are rarer, and when the accounts are settled you come out ahead: 1.75 questions per day on average instead of 2. And no strategy, however clever, will get you below 1.75.

That number, the smallest average count of yes-or-no questions needed to find out what happened, is the entropy of the weather. It is a property of the probabilities alone: the town's weather has 1.75 bits of entropy in the same way that a table has a width.

The surprising part is how far this one idea reaches. The same number is the limit below which no program can compress a file. It is the quantity a neural network is trained to minimise. It measures how much one thing tells you about another, which is how a decision tree picks its splits. And, with a different unit attached, it is the entropy of physics, the one that says a cup of coffee cools down and never spontaneously heats up.

This page builds all of that from the guessing game, one step at a time. Along the way there are four detours that rebuild the tools the main story leans on: logarithms, basic probability, prefix codes and conditional probability. Each is marked as a detour in the table of contents. If one of them is already second nature, skim its practice problems to make sure and move on.

Colour code

The same four objects appear in every formula and figure, always in the same colour: a probability, the surprise of a single outcome (which will turn out to be the same thing as its number of questions, and its codeword length), the entropy, which is an average of surprises, and later a model, a guess at the probabilities that may be wrong, together with what the wrong guess costs or what two variables share, in violet.

2Detour: logarithms

Why this detour

Every formula on this page contains a logarithm, and each time it is there for the same reason: it counts halvings. If that reading of the symbol is solid, the formulas stop looking like formulas.

Start from a question about doubling. You have one bacterium, and the population doubles every hour. How many hours until there are 8? One doubling gives 2, two give 4, three give 8. Three hours. That count is the logarithm:

\[ \log_2 8 = 3 \quad\text{because}\quad 2^3 = 8. \]

In general \(\log_2 x\) answers "2 to the power of what gives \(x\)?" It undoes the exponential, the way subtraction undoes addition. Read backwards it counts halvings instead: start with 8 candidates, cut them in half until one is left, and you have cut \(\log_2 8 = 3\) times. That is the binary search from a minute ago.

\(x\)1248163210241,048,576
\(\log_2 x\)0123451020

Notice how slowly the bottom row grows. A million possibilities are only 20 halvings away.

Numbers below one

Powers of 2 with a negative exponent are fractions: \(2^{-1} = \tfrac12\), \(2^{-2} = \tfrac14\), \(2^{-3} = \tfrac18\). So the logarithm of a fraction is negative:

\[ \log_2 \tfrac14 = -2 \quad\text{because}\quad 2^{-2} = \tfrac14. \]

This matters here because probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1, so their logarithms are always negative or zero. You will see a minus sign put in front to make the result positive, again and again. And \(\log_2 0\) does not exist: no power of 2 is zero. As \(x\) shrinks towards 0, \(\log_2 x\) dives towards \(-\infty\).

Numbers that are not powers of two

What is \(\log_2 3\)? It must lie between 1 and 2, because \(2^1 = 2 \lt 3 \lt 4 = 2^2\). To pin it down, compare big powers. Twelve factors of 3 make \(3^{12} = 531{,}441\), and that sits just above \(2^{19} = 524{,}288\). So twelve triplings are worth a bit more than nineteen doublings, and one tripling is worth a bit more than \(19/12 \approx 1.583\) doublings. The exact value is

\[ \log_2 3 = 1.58496\ldots \]

Fractional exponents are a way of saying exactly this: \(2^{1.585} \approx 3\) means "raise 2 to the 1585th power and you get about the same as 3 to the 1000th".

The three rules

Products become sums. If reaching \(a\) takes \(m\) doublings and reaching \(b\) takes \(n\) doublings, then reaching \(a \cdot b\) takes \(m + n\), because \(2^m \cdot 2^n = 2^{m+n}\):

\[ \log_2 (a \cdot b) = \log_2 a + \log_2 b. \]

This is the rule the whole page rests on. It is why a number between 1 and 16 (4 questions) together with a card suit (4 suits, 2 questions) makes \(16 \cdot 4 = 64\) combinations and \(4 + 2 = 6\) questions.

Reciprocals flip the sign. Since \(a \cdot \tfrac1a = 1\) and \(\log_2 1 = 0\), the product rule gives \(\log_2 a + \log_2 \tfrac1a = 0\), so

\[ \log_2 \tfrac{1}{a} = -\log_2 a. \]

Powers become factors. Multiplying \(a\) by itself \(k\) times adds its logarithm \(k\) times:

\[ \log_2 (a^k) = k \cdot \log_2 a. \]

Other bases

Nothing forces the base to be 2. \(\log_3 x\) counts triplings, \(\log_{10} x\) counts multiplications by ten (roughly, the number of decimal digits). They all measure the same thing in different units, like metres and feet. To convert, divide by the size of the new unit measured in the old one:

\[ \log_b x = \frac{\log_2 x}{\log_2 b}. \]

For example \(\log_3 9 = \log_2 9 / \log_2 3 = 3.170 / 1.585 = 2\). One base gets its own name: the natural logarithm \(\ln x\) has base \(e \approx 2.718\), a number that makes calculus come out clean. You will meet it twice on this page, once in a proof and once in physics. The conversion is \(\log_2 x = \ln x / \ln 2\) with \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\). In Python these are math.log2(x) and math.log(x).

Problem 2.1 · easy

Without a calculator: \(\log_2 64\), \(\log_2 \tfrac{1}{32}\), \(\log_2 1\), and \(\log_2 (8^5)\).

Solution

\(64 = 2^6\), so \(\log_2 64 = 6\).

\(\tfrac1{32} = 2^{-5}\), so the answer is \(-5\). Or by the reciprocal rule: \(-\log_2 32 = -5\).

\(\log_2 1 = 0\): zero doublings are needed to get from 1 to 1.

By the power rule, \(\log_2 (8^5) = 5 \cdot \log_2 8 = 5 \cdot 3 = 15\). Check: \(8^5 = 32{,}768 = 2^{15}\).

Problem 2.2 · medium

You are given only that \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\). Find \(\log_2 6\), \(\log_2 12\), \(\log_2 \tfrac13\) and \(\log_2 \tfrac34\).

Hint

Write each number as a product or quotient of 2s and 3s, then use the product rule.

Solution

\(6 = 2 \cdot 3\), so \(\log_2 6 = 1 + 1.585 = 2.585\).

\(12 = 4 \cdot 3\), so \(\log_2 12 = 2 + 1.585 = 3.585\).

\(\log_2 \tfrac13 = -\log_2 3 = -1.585\).

\(\tfrac34 = 3 \cdot \tfrac14\), so \(\log_2 \tfrac34 = 1.585 + (-2) = -0.415\). It is negative, as it must be for a number below 1, and small, because \(\tfrac34\) is close to 1.

Problem 2.3 · medium

A service has 1,000,000 users. How many bits must a user ID have so that every user can get a different one? Use \(\log_2 10 \approx 3.322\).

Solution

An ID of \(k\) bits can take \(2^k\) different values, so we need the smallest \(k\) with \(2^k \ge 1{,}000{,}000\), which is \(\log_2 1{,}000{,}000\) rounded up.

\(1{,}000{,}000 = 10^6\), so by the power rule \(\log_2 10^6 = 6 \cdot 3.322 = 19.93\). Rounded up: 20 bits. Check: \(2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576\), and \(2^{19} = 524{,}288\) is too few.

The common slip is to round 19.93 to the nearest value and then worry whether it should have been 19: a count of bits must be rounded up, since 19 bits cannot label a million things no matter how close 19.93 is to 19.

3Detour: probability and averages

Why this detour

Entropy is an average taken over a probability distribution. This section fixes what a distribution is, what "independent" means, and how a weighted average works. Nothing else from probability is needed until section 15.

Distributions

A probability distribution is a list of the things that could happen, the outcomes, with a number attached to each one saying how often it happens in the long run. The numbers are at least 0 and they add up to 1. For the mountain town:

tomorrow's reportprobabilityin 1000 days, about
sunny1/2500
cloudy1/4250
rain1/8125
snow1/8125

The notation is \(\pp{P(\text{rain})} = \tfrac18\), or \(\pp{p_i}\) for "the probability of outcome number \(i\)". When several outcomes are lumped together into an event, their probabilities add: the chance that you need an umbrella or a shovel is \(P(\text{rain or snow}) = \tfrac18 + \tfrac18 = \tfrac14\). A distribution that gives every outcome the same probability, like a fair die with \(\tfrac16\) on each face, is called uniform.

Independence

Two things are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Flip a coin and roll a die: the coin does not care what the die shows. For independent things, the probability that both happen is the product of the two probabilities:

\[ P(\text{heads and six}) = P(\text{heads}) \cdot P(\text{six}) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac16 = \tfrac1{12}. \]

The reason is plain counting. Out of 1200 tries, about 600 show heads, and among those 600 the die still shows a six one time in six, so about 100 of the 1200 show both. The product rule is only true under independence. Today's and tomorrow's weather are not independent, and section 15 deals with that case.

Expected value

A game pays you 10 coins when a die shows six and costs you 1 coin otherwise. Is it worth playing? Over 600 rounds you expect about 100 sixes and 500 others, so you collect \(100 \cdot 10 - 500 \cdot 1 = 500\) coins, which is \(500 / 600 \approx 0.83\) coins per round. The same computation without the detour through 600 rounds:

\[ \tfrac16 \cdot 10 + \tfrac56 \cdot (-1) = \tfrac{5}{6} \approx 0.83. \]

That is the expected value: multiply each possible value by the probability of getting it, and add up. It is an average in which likelier outcomes weigh more.

Definition

If outcome \(i\) has probability \(\pp{p_i}\) and comes with a number \(v_i\) (a payout, a cost, a count of questions), the expected value is

\[ \mathbb{E}[v] = \sum_i \pp{p_i} \cdot v_i = \pp{p_1} v_1 + \pp{p_2} v_2 + \dots \]

The symbol \(\sum_i\) only means "add this up over every outcome \(i\)". In code it is sum(p * v for p, v in zip(probabilities, values)).

Two things to keep in mind. The expected value need not be a value you can ever actually get: a fair die has expected value \(3.5\). And it describes the long run, not one round. Entropy will be an expected value, which is why it can be 1.75 questions even though nobody ever asks three quarters of a question.

Problem 3.1 · easy

In the mountain town, assume for this problem that each day's weather is independent of every other day's. What is the probability of three sunny days in a row? Of a snowy day followed by a rainy day?

Solution

Independent, so multiply. Three sunny days: \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac18\).

Snow then rain: \(\tfrac18 \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac1{64}\).

Problem 3.2 · medium

You find out the weather with this fixed plan: first ask "is it sunny?"; if not, ask "is it cloudy?"; if not, ask "is it rain?". Sunny days cost 1 question, cloudy days 2, rain and snow 3 each. What is the expected number of questions per day?

Solution

Each outcome's question count, weighted by how often that outcome happens:

\[ \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75. \]

The tempting wrong answer is the plain average of the four counts, \((1 + 2 + 3 + 3) / 4 = 2.25\). That would be right only if all four kinds of day were equally common. They are not, and the cheap outcome is the common one, which is the entire point of the strategy.

4Counting questions

Back to the game. Before any probabilities get uneven, let us be precise about the simplest case: \(N\) outcomes, all equally likely, and you want the fewest yes-or-no questions that are guaranteed to identify the outcome.

Two separate facts are hiding in this picture.

Halving is enough. With each question you can split the remaining candidates into two equal groups and ask which group the answer is in. After \(k\) questions, \(N / 2^k\) candidates remain. You are done when that reaches 1, which is when \(2^k = N\), which is when \(k = \log_2 N\).

Nothing can do better. A sequence of \(k\) yes/no answers is a string like yes, no, no, yes. There are only \(2^k\) different strings of that length. If two different secrets produced the same string of answers, you could not tell them apart. So \(k\) questions can separate at most \(2^k\) outcomes, and to separate \(N\) of them you need \(2^k \ge N\), that is, \(k \ge \log_2 N\).

Definition: the bit

One bit is the amount of information in the answer to a yes-or-no question whose two answers are equally likely. Identifying one of \(N\) equally likely outcomes takes \(\log_2 N\) bits.

The same word also names a binary digit, a 0 or a 1 in memory. The two meanings are related but not identical: a binary digit can hold up to one bit of information, and holds less if you could have predicted it. Most of this page is about that gap.

What a fraction of a question means

With three equally likely outcomes, say rock, paper, scissors, the formula gives \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\) bits. But you cannot ask 1.585 questions. One question is too few (it separates only two outcomes) and two is the least that works. So is the formula just wrong by rounding?

It is right about something more useful: the price per game when you play many. Suppose your opponent has written down their choice for 12 games in a row. That is one of \(3^{12} = 531{,}441\) possible sequences, all equally likely. Treat the whole sequence as the secret. Since \(2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576\) is more than 531,441, twenty questions identify all twelve choices: \(20 / 12 \approx 1.67\) questions per game, better than 2. Batch more games together and the rounding loss is spread ever thinner, and the price per game sinks towards \(1.585\) without ever going below it.

This is the right way to read every fractional number of bits from here on: it is an exchange rate that becomes exact in bulk.

Problem 4.1 · easy

A card is drawn from a shuffled deck of 52. How many bits of information is the card's identity? How many yes/no questions do you need in the worst case if you ask about one card at a time?

Solution

\(\log_2 52 = \log_2 (4 \cdot 13) = 2 + \log_2 13 \approx 2 + 3.70 = 5.70\) bits.

For a single card you need a whole number of questions, the smallest \(k\) with \(2^k \ge 52\). Since \(2^5 = 32\) is too few and \(2^6 = 64\) is enough, the answer is 6 questions. The remaining \(0.3\) is the rounding loss that batching would recover.

Problem 4.2 · medium

Nine coins look identical, but one is a fake and slightly heavier. You have a balance scale: put some coins on each pan and it tips left, tips right, or stays level. What is the smallest number of weighings that always finds the fake, and why can no scheme do it in fewer?

Hint

A weighing is not a yes/no question. Count its possible answers, and redo the "\(k\) answers can separate at most … outcomes" argument.

Solution

A weighing has three possible results, so \(k\) weighings produce one of \(3^k\) result strings and can separate at most \(3^k\) possibilities. There are 9 possibilities (which coin is fake), so we need \(3^k \ge 9\), that is \(k \ge \log_3 9 = 2\). One weighing cannot do it.

Two weighings suffice, by cutting into thirds instead of halves. Weigh coins 1–3 against coins 4–6. If the left pan sinks, the fake is among 1–3; if the right, among 4–6; if level, among 7–9. Now take the guilty group of three and weigh one of its coins against another: the heavier one is the fake, and if they balance it is the third.

In bits: the fake's identity is \(\log_2 9 \approx 3.17\) bits and a weighing delivers at most \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\) bits, and \(3.17 / 1.585 = 2\). The common wrong answer is 4 weighings (from \(\lceil \log_2 9 \rceil\)), which treats the scale as if it answered yes or no.

5The weather station

Now the uneven case, with a story to make the stakes concrete. A weather station on a mountain sends one report per day down to the valley: sunny, cloudy, rain or snow. The link is a satellite uplink billed per bit, so the station wants to send as few bits as possible. Years of records give the probabilities from the last section: sunny \(\pp{\tfrac12}\), cloudy \(\pp{\tfrac14}\), rain \(\pp{\tfrac18}\), snow \(\pp{\tfrac18}\).

Sending bits and answering questions are the same activity. If the valley and the station agree beforehand on a list of questions, the station can simply transmit the answers, 1 for yes and 0 for no. So "fewest bits per report" and "fewest questions per day" are one problem.

The naive plan

Four outcomes, so halve twice. Two questions, two bits, every day.

Where it goes wrong

Look at the first question, "sunny or cloudy?". The answer is yes three days out of four. A question whose answer you can usually guess is not pulling its weight. The halving argument of the last section worked because each question split the candidates into two equally likely halves, and here the halves are equal in the number of outcomes but not in probability: \(\tfrac34\) against \(\tfrac14\).

So let us halve the probability instead of the list. "Is it sunny?" is a true 50/50 question. If the answer is no, what remains is cloudy with \(\tfrac14\) and rain-or-snow with \(\tfrac14\), so "is it cloudy?" is again 50/50 among what is left. And then rain against snow, 50/50 once more.

Problem 3.2 already computed the cost of this plan:

\[ \pp{\tfrac12} \cdot \ss{1} + \pp{\tfrac14} \cdot \ss{2} + \pp{\tfrac18} \cdot \ss{3} + \pp{\tfrac18} \cdot \ss{3} = 1.75 \text{ questions per day.} \]

A saving of an eighth of the bill, from nothing but asking in a better order. Now put the two columns next to each other:

reportprobabilityquestions needed
sunny1/21
cloudy1/42
rain1/83
snow1/83

An outcome with probability \(\tfrac12\) took 1 question, \(\tfrac14\) took 2, \(\tfrac18\) took 3. Each outcome needs exactly as many questions as the number of halvings it takes to get from 1 down to its probability. That is not a coincidence of this example, and the next section shows why.

6The surprise of one outcome

Forget strategies for a moment and ask a more basic question. When you learn that something happened, how much have you learned? "It is sunny in the Sahara" tells you almost nothing. "It is snowing in the Sahara" is news. The rarer the event, the more you learn from hearing that it occurred. We want a number for that: the surprise \(\ss{s}(\pp{p})\) of an event with probability \(\pp{p}\).

Rather than guess a formula, write down what any sensible measure must do, and see what is left.

  1. Certain events carry no surprise. \(\ss{s}(1) = 0\).
  2. Rarer means more surprising. If \(\pp{p}\) gets smaller, \(\ss{s}(\pp{p})\) gets bigger.
  3. Independent surprises add. If you learn two independent things, the total you have learned is the sum of the two. Hearing "the coin came up heads" and "the die shows six" should be worth the first piece of news plus the second.

The third requirement does the real work. Two independent events with probabilities \(\pp{p}\) and \(\pp{q}\) happen together with probability \(\pp{p \cdot q}\) (section 3). So requirement 3 says

\[ \ss{s}(\pp{p \cdot q}) = \ss{s}(\pp{p}) + \ss{s}(\pp{q}). \]

A function that turns products into sums. Section 2 had exactly one family of those: logarithms. Let us check that the requirement really forces a logarithm, step by step.

Step 1: pick the unit. Declare that a fair coin flip is worth one unit: \(\ss{s}(\tfrac12) = 1\). This is a choice, like choosing metres.

Step 2: powers of one half. Two heads in a row have probability \(\tfrac14 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12\), so by the addition rule \(\ss{s}(\tfrac14) = \ss{s}(\tfrac12) + \ss{s}(\tfrac12) = 2\). Three in a row: \(\ss{s}(\tfrac18) = 3\). In general \(k\) heads in a row give \(\ss{s}(2^{-k}) = k\).

Step 3: roots. What about a probability like \(\pp{p} = 2^{-1/2} \approx 0.707\)? Two independent events of that probability happen together with probability \(2^{-1/2} \cdot 2^{-1/2} = \tfrac12\). So \(\ss{s}(\pp{p}) + \ss{s}(\pp{p}) = \ss{s}(\tfrac12) = 1\), which forces \(\ss{s}(2^{-1/2}) = \tfrac12\). The same argument with \(m\) copies gives \(\ss{s}(2^{-1/m}) = \tfrac1m\), and combining with step 2, \(\ss{s}(2^{-k/m}) = \tfrac km\) for every fraction \(\tfrac km\).

Step 4: everything else. Any probability \(\pp{p}\) can be written as \(2^{-x}\) for some real number \(x \ge 0\). Squeeze \(x\) between two fractions that are as close together as you like. Because rarer means more surprising (requirement 2), \(\ss{s}(\pp{p})\) is squeezed between those same two fractions, so it must equal \(x\). And what is \(x\)? It is the number with \(2^{-x} = \pp{p}\), which is \(-\log_2 \pp{p}\).

Definition: surprise

The surprise (also called self-information or surprisal) of an outcome with probability \(\pp{p}\) is

\[ \ss{s}(\pp{p}) = \log_2 \frac{1}{\pp{p}} = -\log_2 \pp{p} \quad\text{bits.} \]

In words: the number of times you must halve 1 to get down to \(\pp{p}\). Equivalently, an event of surprise \(\ss{s}\) is exactly as unlikely as \(\ss{s}\) fair coin flips all landing heads.

This explains the table at the end of the last section. In the lopsided plan every question was a 50/50 question, worth exactly one bit. An outcome of probability \(\tfrac18\) carries 3 bits of surprise, so it takes three such questions to deliver it. The number of questions an outcome needs is its surprise.

The choice in step 1 was only a unit. Declaring instead that an event of probability \(1/e\) is worth one unit gives \(\ss{s}(\pp{p}) = -\ln \pp{p}\), measured in nats. One nat is \(1 / \ln 2 \approx 1.443\) bits. Machine-learning libraries usually report nats, because the natural logarithm is the convenient one for calculus. Nothing else changes.

Problem 6.1 · easy

How surprising is rolling a six with a fair die? Rolling two sixes with two dice? (Use \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\).)

Solution

One six: \(\log_2 6 = \log_2 2 + \log_2 3 = 2.585\) bits.

Two sixes: the probability is \(\tfrac1{36}\), and \(\log_2 36 = 2 \cdot \log_2 6 = 5.17\) bits. Or skip the multiplication altogether: the dice are independent, so the surprises add, \(2.585 + 2.585\). That shortcut is requirement 3 doing its job.

Problem 6.2 · medium

A 6-of-49 lottery has \(13{,}983{,}816\) equally likely tickets. How many bits of surprise is a jackpot? How many fair coin flips in a row landing heads would be about as unlikely? And how surprising is it to not win?

Solution

\(\log_2 13{,}983{,}816 \approx 23.74\) bits. That is a little less unlikely than 24 heads in a row (\(2^{24} = 16{,}777{,}216\)) and more unlikely than 23.

Not winning has probability \(1 - 1/13{,}983{,}816\), and \(-\log_2 (1 - 1/13{,}983{,}816) \approx 0.0000001\) bits. Losing the lottery is not news.

Notice the asymmetry: the two outcomes of the same draw carry wildly different amounts of information. A single number that describes the draw as a whole will have to average them, which is the next section.

Problem 6.3 · medium

Requirement 3 was about independent events. Show with a concrete example that surprises do not simply add when the events are dependent.

Solution

Take one die roll and the two events "the die shows six" and "the die shows an even number". Their surprises are \(\log_2 6 = 2.585\) bits and \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit, which add to 3.585 bits.

But both happening together is just "the die shows six", with surprise 2.585 bits. Once you know it is a six, the news that it is even is worth nothing, because you could have worked it out. The second message was entirely redundant. Section 16 turns this redundancy into a quantity of its own, the mutual information.

7Entropy: the average surprise

Surprise belongs to a single outcome. The station does not pay for one outcome, though. It pays every day, for whatever comes, so the number it cares about is the surprise it should expect on a typical day. Section 3 has the tool for "typical": the expected value. Weight each outcome's surprise by how often that outcome occurs.

Definition: entropy

The entropy of a distribution with probabilities \(\pp{p_1}, \pp{p_2}, \dots, \pp{p_n}\) is its expected surprise:

\[ \hh{H} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \pp{p_i} \cdot \ss{\log_2 \frac{1}{\pp{p_i}}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \pp{p_i} \log_2 \pp{p_i} \quad\text{bits.} \]

Each term is how often times how surprising. When the distribution belongs to a random quantity called \(X\), such as tomorrow's weather, the entropy is written \(\hh{H}(X)\); when the probabilities themselves are named \(p\), it is written \(\hh{H}(p)\).

For the mountain town, one row per outcome:

The same 1.75 as the lopsided question plan, and now we can see why they had to agree: that plan spent on each outcome exactly its surprise, so its average cost is the average surprise.

Look at the last column. Snow is the most surprising outcome, yet it contributes less than sunny does, because it so seldom happens. And an outcome that never happens contributes nothing at all, however surprising it would be.

A picture of the sum

A sum of products can be drawn as a row of rectangles. Give each outcome a rectangle whose width is its probability and whose height is its surprise. Its area is then its contribution, and the total area is the entropy. Because the widths add up to 1, the entropy is also the average height of the skyline.

The outcome that never happens

The formula has a wrinkle when some \(\pp{p_i} = 0\): the term is \(0 \cdot \log_2 \tfrac10\), zero times infinity. The demo shows what the right reading is. As a probability shrinks, the surprise grows, but only logarithmically, while the width shrinks linearly, and linear wins. For \(\pp{p} = \tfrac1{1024}\) the surprise is 10 bits and the contribution is \(10/1024 \approx 0.0098\); for \(\pp{p} = 2^{-20}\) it is \(20 / 1{,}048{,}576 \approx 0.00002\). So the convention is:

Convention

In entropy sums, \(0 \cdot \log_2 0\) counts as \(0\). Impossible outcomes are simply skipped. This is why the code below has if probability > 0; without it, math.log2(0) raises a ValueError.

In code

Here \(\pp{p_i}\) is probability, \(\ss{\log_2(1/p_i)}\) is surprise_bits(probability), and the sum over \(i\) is the generator expression.

import math


def surprise_bits(probability: float) -> float:
    """How surprising an outcome of this probability is, in bits."""
    return -math.log2(probability)


def entropy_bits(probabilities: list[float]) -> float:
    """Average surprise of a distribution, in bits. Impossible outcomes contribute 0."""
    return sum(
        probability * surprise_bits(probability)
        for probability in probabilities
        if probability > 0
    )

8The shape of entropy

A formula is easier to trust once you have watched it move. The simplest distribution with anything to vary is a coin that lands heads with probability \(\pp{p}\) and tails with \(\pp{1-p}\). Its entropy is a function of the one number \(\pp{p}\), called the binary entropy function:

\[ \hh{H}(\pp{p}) = \pp{p} \log_2 \frac{1}{\pp{p}} + \pp{(1-p)} \log_2 \frac{1}{\pp{1-p}}. \]

Three features of this curve hold for every distribution, not only for coins.

Entropy is zero exactly when there is nothing to learn

Every term \(\pp{p_i} \cdot \ss{s_i}\) is a non-negative number times a non-negative number, so \(\hh{H} \ge 0\). The sum is zero only if every term is zero, and a term is zero only when \(\pp{p_i} = 0\) (never happens) or \(\ss{s_i} = 0\) (always happens). So \(\hh{H} = 0\) exactly when one outcome has probability 1.

Entropy is largest when all outcomes are equally likely

For \(n\) outcomes, \(\hh{H} \le \log_2 n\), with equality only for the uniform distribution. The rectangle demo makes this plausible, and here is the intuition: a lopsided distribution is one where you can make a decent guess, and being able to guess is the opposite of being uncertain. A proof needs one more tool, and it comes in section 12, where this fact drops out in two lines.

Independent uncertainties add

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, the entropy of the pair is the sum: \(\hh{H}(X, Y) = \hh{H}(X) + \hh{H}(Y)\). This is the property that makes entropy a measure, something you can add up like length. It is inherited directly from surprise. Take any pair of outcomes \((x, y)\). By independence its probability is \(\pp{p(x)} \cdot \pp{p(y)}\), so its surprise is the sum \(\ss{s(x)} + \ss{s(y)}\). The expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, so the average surprise of the pair is the average surprise of \(X\) plus the average surprise of \(Y\). Spelled out with the sums:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hh{H}(X, Y) &= \sum_x \sum_y \pp{p(x) p(y)} \, \bigl( \ss{s(x)} + \ss{s(y)} \bigr) \\ &= \sum_x \pp{p(x)} \ss{s(x)} \underbrace{\sum_y \pp{p(y)}}_{=\,1} \;+\; \sum_y \pp{p(y)} \ss{s(y)} \underbrace{\sum_x \pp{p(x)}}_{=\,1} \\ &= \hh{H}(X) + \hh{H}(Y). \end{aligned} \]

So ten independent fair coin flips have 10 bits of entropy, and a week of the mountain town's weather, if the days were independent, would have \(7 \cdot 1.75 = 12.25\) bits.

9Detour: prefix codes

Why this detour

So far "1.75 bits" has meant questions in a game. To claim that entropy is the limit of compression we need to talk about actual strings of 0s and 1s sent down a wire, and about which sets of strings can be decoded at all.

A code assigns to each symbol a string of bits, its codeword. A message is encoded by writing the codewords one after another with nothing in between: no spaces, no commas, since the wire carries only 0s and 1s.

The simplest kind is a fixed-length code: every codeword has the same number of bits. ASCII is one. For the four weather reports, two bits each: sunny 00, cloudy 01, rain 10, snow 11. Decoding is trivial, because you chop the stream into pairs. That is the balanced question plan of section 5, and it costs 2 bits per report.

To do better we want short codewords for common symbols, as Morse code gives E a single dot. So try this variable-length code: sunny 0, cloudy 1, rain 01, snow 10. Short indeed. Now the valley receives

0110

Is that sunny-cloudy-cloudy-sunny? Rain-snow? Sunny-cloudy-snow? All three encode to the same four bits. The code is useless: without separators, the receiver cannot tell where one codeword ends and the next begins.

The prefix-free property

The ambiguity arose because the codeword for sunny, 0, is the beginning of the codeword for rain, 01. Having read a 0, the receiver cannot know whether a codeword just ended. The cure is to forbid that situation.

Definition: prefix code

A code is prefix-free, or a prefix code, if no codeword is the beginning of another codeword. Such a code can be decoded instantly: the moment the bits read so far form a codeword, that codeword is the only possibility, so the receiver emits the symbol and starts afresh.

Here is one for the weather: sunny 0, cloudy 10, rain 110, snow 111. No codeword starts with 0 except sunny itself; none starts with 10 except cloudy; and 110 and 111 differ in their last bit.

Codes are trees, and trees are question plans

Every prefix code can be drawn as a binary tree. Start at the root; each bit read is a step down, 0 to the left and 1 to the right; the symbols sit at the leaves. Being prefix-free means precisely that symbols sit only at leaves: if a symbol sat at an inner node, its codeword would be a prefix of everything below it.

That comparison is the bridge between the first half of this page and the second. A plan of yes/no questions is a prefix code: the codeword of an outcome is the list of answers that lead to it. The number of questions an outcome needs is the length of its codeword. And the expected number of questions is the code's average length,

\[ L = \sum_i \pp{p_i} \cdot \ss{\ell_i}, \]

where \(\ss{\ell_i}\) is the length in bits of the codeword for outcome \(i\). For the code above, \(L = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 1.75\) bits per report.

Solution

Yes, it is uniquely decodable, but not instantly. Reverse every codeword and you get 0, 10, 11, which is a prefix code. So any message can be decoded unambiguously by reading it from the end.

0111 can only be 01·11. 01111 can only be 0·11·11. Reading from the front, after 01 you cannot yet tell which of these you are in: you have to count the 1s that follow, all the way to the end of the run, before the first symbol is known. A prefix code never makes you wait.

So prefix codes are a proper subset of uniquely decodable codes. It turns out (McMillan's theorem, section 10) that the larger family offers no shorter codeword lengths, so nothing is lost by insisting on prefix codes.

10The codeword budget

If short codewords are good, why not make all of them short? Because they compete. In the tree picture, choosing the codeword 0 for sunny uses up the entire left half of the tree: nothing else may live below it. A codeword 10 uses up a quarter. Short codewords are expensive, and there is only one tree to spend.

The price list is exact. A codeword of length \(\ss{\ell}\) blocks a fraction \(2^{-\ss{\ell}}\) of the tree: half for length 1, a quarter for length 2, an eighth for length 3. A set of lengths is affordable when the fractions fit into the whole.

Kraft's inequality

A prefix code with codeword lengths \(\ss{\ell_1}, \dots, \ss{\ell_n}\) exists if and only if

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} 2^{-\ss{\ell_i}} \le 1. \]

Why it is true

A prefix code cannot overspend. Let \(\ell_{\max}\) be the longest codeword length, and picture the complete binary tree down to that depth. Its bottom row has \(2^{\ell_{\max}}\) nodes. A codeword of length \(\ss{\ell}\) is a node at depth \(\ss{\ell}\), and it has \(2^{\ell_{\max} - \ss{\ell}}\) descendants in the bottom row (each step down doubles the count). Two different codewords cannot share a bottom-row descendant: if they did, both would lie on the single path from the root to that descendant, so one would be an ancestor of the other, that is, a prefix. So the sets of descendants are disjoint, and together they cannot exceed the row:

\[ \sum_i 2^{\ell_{\max} - \ss{\ell_i}} \le 2^{\ell_{\max}}. \]

Divide both sides by \(2^{\ell_{\max}}\) and Kraft's inequality appears.

Any affordable set of lengths can be realised. Sort the lengths from shortest to longest and hand out codewords greedily: for each length, take the leftmost node at that depth that is not below a codeword already chosen. Why is one always available? In the bar picture, the codewords handed out so far occupy a solid stretch at the left end of the bar, and since their sizes are all multiples of the current, smaller, piece size, that stretch ends on a boundary where a piece of the current size can start. As long as the total stays within 1, the piece fits. The demo above does exactly this, and prints the code it finds.

A stronger fact, McMillan's theorem, says that every uniquely decodable code, prefix-free or not, satisfies the same inequality. So the code from problem 9.3 could not have had better lengths than some prefix code, and from now on "code" means prefix code.

Spending the budget well

Now the station's problem has a clean form. Choose lengths \(\ss{\ell_i}\) to make the average \(\sum_i \pp{p_i} \ss{\ell_i}\) as small as possible, subject to the budget \(\sum_i 2^{-\ss{\ell_i}} \le 1\).

Think of \(2^{-\ss{\ell_i}}\) as the share of the tree that outcome \(i\) buys. The shares must sum to at most 1. Probabilities also sum to 1. The natural move is to give each outcome a share equal to its probability: the outcome that happens half of the time gets half of the tree. Setting \(2^{-\ss{\ell_i}} = \pp{p_i}\) and solving for the length gives

\[ \ss{\ell_i} = \log_2 \frac{1}{\pp{p_i}}. \]

The ideal codeword length of an outcome is its surprise. With these lengths the average length is exactly the entropy. Section 13 proves that no other way of dividing the budget does better; for now, the demo lets you convince yourself for one example.

There is a catch. For the mountain town all the surprises were whole numbers, because all the probabilities were powers of \(\tfrac12\). For a probability of \(0.4\) the ideal length is \(1.32\) bits, and a codeword cannot have 1.32 bits. Something has to give, and the next section is about giving up as little as possible.

11Huffman's algorithm

The station is moved to a town with five kinds of report and less convenient probabilities: sunny \(\pp{0.4}\), cloudy \(\pp{0.2}\), rain \(\pp{0.2}\), fog \(\pp{0.1}\), snow \(\pp{0.1}\). The entropy is \(\hh{2.122}\) bits, and the ideal codeword lengths are \(\ss{1.32}\), \(\ss{2.32}\), \(\ss{2.32}\), \(\ss{3.32}\), \(\ss{3.32}\). None is a whole number. What is the best code we can actually build?

A first attempt: round up

Round every ideal length up to the next whole number: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4. Rounding up only makes codewords cheaper in budget terms, so Kraft's inequality still holds and a code exists. This is called a Shannon code. Its average length is \(0.4 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 3 + 0.2 \cdot 3 + 0.1 \cdot 4 + 0.1 \cdot 4 = 2.8\) bits. That is worse than the 3 bits of a fixed-length code by only a little, and a look at the budget shows why: the Kraft sum is \(\tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \tfrac18 + \tfrac1{16} + \tfrac1{16} = 0.625\). More than a third of the tree is unspent, and problem 10.2 showed that unspent budget means codewords that could be shorter.

The key insight: build from the bottom

The question plans of section 5 were built from the top: find a first question that splits the probability in half. With these numbers no clean split exists, and top-down splitting (the Shannon–Fano method) can end up suboptimal. David Huffman's idea, in 1951, was to start at the other end, with the one thing we can be sure of.

The two rarest symbols should have the two longest codewords, and they may as well differ only in the last bit. In tree terms: the two rarest symbols are siblings at the deepest level. So join them under a common parent right away. From then on the pair behaves like a single symbol whose probability is the sum of the two, because "fog or snow" is itself an outcome, with probability \(0.2\). Now there are four symbols instead of five, and the same reasoning applies again.

Huffman's algorithm
  1. Make a leaf for every symbol, labelled with its probability.
  2. Take the two nodes with the smallest probabilities. Join them under a new parent whose probability is their sum.
  3. Repeat step 2 until one node is left. It is the root.
  4. Label the two branches below every parent 0 and 1. A symbol's codeword is the sequence of labels on the path from the root down to its leaf.

The result has lengths 2, 2, 2, 3, 3 and an average of \(0.4 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 2 + 0.1 \cdot 3 + 0.1 \cdot 3 = 2.2\) bits per report. That is within \(0.08\) bits of the entropy \(\hh{2.122}\), and far better than the Shannon code's 2.8.

In code

A min-heap hands us the two smallest nodes in \(O(\log n)\) each. The dataclass is ordered by probability first, with creation_order as a tie-breaker so that the heap never tries to compare two symbols or two subtrees.

from __future__ import annotations

import heapq
import math
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass(order=True)
class Node:
    probability: float
    creation_order: int  # tie-breaker, so the heap never has to compare symbols
    symbol: str | None = field(default=None, compare=False)
    zero_child: Node | None = field(default=None, compare=False)
    one_child: Node | None = field(default=None, compare=False)


def build_huffman_tree(symbol_probabilities: dict[str, float]) -> Node:
    heap: list[Node] = [
        Node(probability, creation_order, symbol)
        for creation_order, (symbol, probability) in enumerate(symbol_probabilities.items())
    ]
    heapq.heapify(heap)
    next_creation_order = len(heap)
    while len(heap) > 1:
        rarest = heapq.heappop(heap)
        second_rarest = heapq.heappop(heap)
        merged = Node(
            rarest.probability + second_rarest.probability,
            next_creation_order,
            zero_child=second_rarest,
            one_child=rarest,
        )
        next_creation_order += 1
        heapq.heappush(heap, merged)
    return heap[0]


def read_codewords(node: Node, prefix: str = "") -> dict[str, str]:
    if node.symbol is not None:
        return {node.symbol: prefix or "0"}
    assert node.zero_child is not None and node.one_child is not None
    return read_codewords(node.zero_child, prefix + "0") | read_codewords(node.one_child, prefix + "1")


def entropy_bits(probabilities: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(-p * math.log2(p) for p in probabilities if p > 0)

Why the greedy choice is safe

The argument has two steps, and each is short enough to give in full.

Step 1: some optimal tree has the two rarest symbols as deepest siblings. Take any optimal tree. By problem 10.2 it has no node with a single child, so at its deepest level there are two sibling leaves; call the symbols sitting there \(x\) and \(y\). Let \(a\) be one of the two rarest symbols. If \(a\) is not already one of those two, swap \(a\) with \(x\). Symbol \(a\) moves down by \(d = \text{depth}(x) - \text{depth}(a) \ge 0\) levels and \(x\) moves up by the same amount, so the average length changes by

\[ \pp{p_a} \cdot d - \pp{p_x} \cdot d = (\pp{p_a} - \pp{p_x}) \cdot d \le 0, \]

because \(a\) is among the rarest, so \(\pp{p_a} \le \pp{p_x}\). The swap cannot have made the tree worse, and the tree was optimal, so it is still optimal. Do the same for the other rarest symbol and \(y\).

Step 2: merging reduces the problem to a smaller one. Take a tree in which the rarest symbols \(a\) and \(b\) are siblings, and compare it with the same tree with both leaves cut off, their parent becoming a leaf for the merged symbol "\(a\) or \(b\)". The codewords for \(a\) and \(b\) are exactly one bit longer than the merged symbol's, and they are used with total probability \(\pp{p_a + p_b}\). So

\[ L_{\text{full tree}} = L_{\text{merged tree}} + (\pp{p_a} + \pp{p_b}). \]

The added term does not depend on the shape of the tree. So the full tree is as good as possible exactly when the merged tree is as good as possible for the smaller set of symbols, and that smaller problem is solved the same way. With two symbols left the answer is obvious: one bit each.

Edge case: ties

When probabilities tie, the "two smallest" are not unique, and different choices give different trees. For this example another valid Huffman tree has lengths 1, 2, 3, 4, 4. Its average is also 2.2. Every Huffman tree is optimal, but the optimal code is not unique, so two correct implementations can print different codewords.

Closing the last gap

Huffman's code is the best possible code that gives each symbol its own codeword. Yet it can still sit far above the entropy. The extreme case is a two-symbol source. Suppose the weather is dry \(\pp{90\%}\) of days and wet \(\pp{10\%}\). The entropy is \(\hh{0.469}\) bits, but any code must spend at least one whole bit per report, more than double.

Problem 11.3 · harder

Someone claims: "Huffman's algorithm is optimal, so zip could not do better than a Huffman code over bytes." What is wrong with this, apart from the block effect just discussed?

Solution

Huffman is optimal among codes that assign one fixed codeword to each symbol, for a source whose symbols are independent draws from one fixed distribution. Real data violates the second assumption badly. In English text a q is almost always followed by u; given the previous letter, the next one is much more predictable than the overall letter frequencies suggest.

A compressor that uses context, such as repeated substrings (LZ77, as in zip) or a model of the next symbol given the preceding ones, is in effect coding against a different, sharper probability distribution with lower entropy. The entropy bound is never violated; the bound itself depends on the probabilistic model, and a better model has a lower bound. Sections 16 and 18 come back to this.

12The wrong code: cross-entropy and KL divergence

A second station is installed in a town on the coast. Nobody bothers to study the local weather; the new station is shipped with the mountain station's code: sunny 0, cloudy 10, rain 110, snow 111. But on the coast it rains half of the time: rain \(\pp{\tfrac12}\), cloudy \(\pp{\tfrac14}\), sunny \(\pp{\tfrac18}\), snow \(\pp{\tfrac18}\).

The code still works, in the sense that every report can be sent and decoded. It is just badly matched: the most common report now has one of the longest codewords. What does the station pay per day? The frequencies are the coast's, the lengths are the mountain's:

\[ \underbrace{\pp{\tfrac18} \cdot \qq{1}}_{\text{sunny}} + \underbrace{\pp{\tfrac14} \cdot \qq{2}}_{\text{cloudy}} + \underbrace{\pp{\tfrac12} \cdot \qq{3}}_{\text{rain}} + \underbrace{\pp{\tfrac18} \cdot \qq{3}}_{\text{snow}} = 2.5 \text{ bits.} \]

The coast's weather is, as a list of probabilities, a reshuffling of the mountain's, so its entropy is the same \(\hh{1.75}\) bits and a code designed for it would cost \(1.75\). The mismatched code costs \(2.5\): worse even than the fixed-length code's 2. The station pays \(\ii{0.75}\) bits a day for a belief about the weather that is false.

Two distributions are now in play, and it pays to name them. There is the truth \(p\): how often things actually happen. And there is the model \(q\): the distribution the code was designed for, a belief about the world. A code built for \(\qq{q}\) gives outcome \(i\) a length of \(\qq{\log_2 (1/q_i)}\), the surprise that someone who believes \(\qq{q}\) would feel. But the outcomes arrive with frequencies \(\pp{p_i}\).

Definitions

The cross-entropy of a model \(q\) relative to the truth \(p\) is the expected surprise of someone who believes \(\qq{q}\) while living in \(\pp{p}\):

\[ H(\pp{p}, \qq{q}) = \sum_i \pp{p_i} \, \qq{\log_2 \frac{1}{q_i}}. \]

The Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL divergence, or relative entropy) is the part of that which is wasted, the excess over what the truth requires:

\[ \ii{D(p \,\|\, q)} = H(\pp{p}, \qq{q}) - \hh{H(p)} = \sum_i \pp{p_i} \log_2 \frac{\pp{p_i}}{\qq{q_i}}. \]

The last form comes from subtracting term by term: \(\log_2 \tfrac1{q_i} - \log_2 \tfrac1{p_i} = \log_2 \tfrac{p_i}{q_i}\).

The definitions are rearranged into a sentence worth remembering:

\[ \underbrace{H(\pp{p}, \qq{q})}_{\text{what you pay}} \;=\; \underbrace{\hh{H(p)}}_{\text{what the world costs}} \;+\; \underbrace{\ii{D(p \,\|\, q)}}_{\text{what your wrong belief costs}}. \]

Gibbs' inequality: a wrong belief never pays

The demo suggests a law: \(\ii{D(p\,\|\,q)} \ge 0\), with equality only when the model is exactly right. It is not obvious. For a single outcome the model's code may well be shorter than the ideal one (sunny, in the coast example: 1 bit instead of 3). The claim is that such savings are always more than paid for elsewhere, because shortening one codeword lengthens others (Kraft's budget) and the true frequencies weight the losses more heavily than the gains.

A short detour inside the proof

The proof needs a single fact about the natural logarithm: \(\ln x \le x - 1\) for every \(x \gt 0\), with equality only at \(x = 1\). It is the one place on this page where calculus is used, and a picture carries most of it.

Why does the curve stay below? Both pass through the point \((1, 0)\). The line climbs at a steady rate of 1: one unit up for each unit to the right. The steepness of \(\ln x\) at the point \(x\) is \(1/x\) (this is the calculus fact, the derivative of the natural logarithm, and it is the reason base \(e\) is called natural). To the right of 1, \(1/x\) is less than 1, so the curve climbs more slowly than the line and falls behind. To the left of 1, \(1/x\) is more than 1, so walking leftwards the curve drops faster than the line does, and again ends up below. Either way, away from \(x = 1\), the curve is strictly under the line.

Now the proof. We show that \(-\ii{D(p\,\|\,q)} \le 0\). Flip the fraction inside the logarithm to absorb the minus sign, convert to natural logarithms with \(\log_2 x = \ln x / \ln 2\), and apply the fact to \(x = q_i / p_i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} -\ii{D(p\,\|\,q)} &= \sum_i \pp{p_i} \log_2 \frac{\qq{q_i}}{\pp{p_i}} = \frac{1}{\ln 2} \sum_i \pp{p_i} \ln \frac{\qq{q_i}}{\pp{p_i}} \\ &\le \frac{1}{\ln 2} \sum_i \pp{p_i} \left( \frac{\qq{q_i}}{\pp{p_i}} - 1 \right) && \text{since } \ln x \le x - 1 \\ &= \frac{1}{\ln 2} \Bigl( \sum_i \qq{q_i} - \sum_i \pp{p_i} \Bigr) && \text{multiplying out} \\ &\le \frac{1}{\ln 2} \,(1 - 1) = 0. \end{aligned} \]

The sums run over the outcomes with \(\pp{p_i} \gt 0\). Over those, the \(\pp{p_i}\) sum to exactly 1 and the \(\qq{q_i}\) sum to at most 1, which gives the last line. Equality requires \(q_i / p_i = 1\) in every term, that is, \(\qq{q} = \pp{p}\).

Gibbs' inequality

\(\ii{D(p\,\|\,q)} \ge 0\), with equality exactly when \(\qq{q} = \pp{p}\). Equivalently \(H(\pp{p}, \qq{q}) \ge \hh{H(p)}\): of all beliefs you could hold, the truth is the one that leaves you least surprised on average.

A debt repaid: the uniform distribution has the most entropy

Section 8 claimed \(\hh{H(p)} \le \log_2 n\) for any distribution over \(n\) outcomes. Take the model to be the uniform distribution, \(\qq{q_i} = \tfrac1n\) for all \(i\). Then every codeword length is \(\log_2 n\), so the cross-entropy is \(\sum_i \pp{p_i} \log_2 n = \log_2 n\) regardless of \(\pp{p}\). Gibbs' inequality says

\[ \ii{D(p \,\|\, \text{uniform})} = \log_2 n - \hh{H(p)} \ge 0, \]

which is the claim, with equality only when \(\pp{p}\) is itself uniform. It also gives the gap a meaning: the distance of the entropy from its maximum is what a fixed-length code wastes.

Three things to know about KL divergence

It is not symmetric. \(D(p\,\|\,q)\) and \(D(q\,\|\,p)\) answer different questions: "reality is \(p\), I believe \(q\)" against "reality is \(q\), I believe \(p\)". The weighting comes from whichever is the truth. So KL divergence is not a distance, despite often being called one.

It can be infinite. If the model says \(\qq{q_i} = 0\) for something that actually happens (\(\pp{p_i} \gt 0\)), the model has no codeword for it at all, and the cost is \(\log_2 \tfrac10 = \infty\). Declaring something impossible and being wrong is the costliest mistake there is. This is why the demo's green sliders do not go down to zero.

The other direction is harmless. If \(\pp{p_i} = 0\) and \(\qq{q_i} \gt 0\), the model reserves budget for something that never happens. That is wasteful, and shows up as longer codewords for everything else, but the term itself is \(0\).

import math


def entropy_bits(true_probabilities: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(-p * math.log2(p) for p in true_probabilities if p > 0)


def cross_entropy_bits(true_probabilities: list[float], model_probabilities: list[float]) -> float:
    """Average bits per outcome when reality follows `true_probabilities`
    but the code was designed for `model_probabilities`."""
    return sum(
        -true_probability * math.log2(model_probability)
        for true_probability, model_probability in zip(true_probabilities, model_probabilities, strict=True)
        if true_probability > 0
    )


def kl_divergence_bits(true_probabilities: list[float], model_probabilities: list[float]) -> float:
    return cross_entropy_bits(true_probabilities, model_probabilities) - entropy_bits(true_probabilities)

The mountain town, \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)\), is coded with the fixed-length 2-bit code, which corresponds to the model \(q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\). Compute \(H(p, q)\) and \(D(p\,\|\,q)\).

Solution

Every outcome has model surprise \(\log_2 4 = 2\), so \(H(p, q) = (\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \tfrac18) \cdot 2 = 2\) bits.

Problem 12.2 · medium

Let \(a = (0.5, 0.5)\) be a fair coin and \(b = (0.9, 0.1)\) a biased one. Compute \(D(a\,\|\,b)\) and \(D(b\,\|\,a)\). Use \(\log_2 0.9 \approx -0.152\), \(\log_2 0.1 \approx -3.322\), and \(H(b) = 0.469\). Which mistake is worse: believing a fair coin is biased, or believing a biased coin is fair?

Solution

Truth fair, belief biased. Cross-entropy: \(0.5 \cdot 0.152 + 0.5 \cdot 3.322 = 1.737\). Entropy of the truth: 1. So \(D(a\,\|\,b) = 0.737\) bits.

13Why entropy is the limit

Everything is now in place to prove the claim that has been made since section 1: no code beats the entropy, and some code comes within one bit of it.

Shannon's source coding theorem, for symbol codes

Let \(\pp{p}\) be a distribution with entropy \(\hh{H}\).

  1. Every prefix code has average length \(L \ge \hh{H}\).
  2. There is a prefix code with \(L \lt \hh{H} + 1\).

Part 1: no code beats the entropy

Take any prefix code, with lengths \(\ss{\ell_i}\). The plan is to recognise it as the ideal code for some model \(\qq{q}\), and then let Gibbs' inequality do the work.

Let \(c = \sum_i 2^{-\ss{\ell_i}}\) be the amount of budget the code spends. By Kraft's inequality \(c \le 1\). Define a model by scaling the shares so that they sum to exactly 1:

\[ \qq{q_i} = \frac{2^{-\ss{\ell_i}}}{c}. \]

Solve this for the length. Multiply by \(c\): \(2^{-\ss{\ell_i}} = c \, \qq{q_i}\). Take \(\log_2\) of both sides: \(-\ss{\ell_i} = \log_2 c + \log_2 \qq{q_i}\). Flip the signs:

\[ \ss{\ell_i} = \qq{\log_2 \frac{1}{q_i}} + \log_2 \frac{1}{c}. \]

Now compute the average length, using \(\sum_i \pp{p_i} = 1\) for the second term:

\[ \begin{aligned} L = \sum_i \pp{p_i} \ss{\ell_i} &= \sum_i \pp{p_i} \, \qq{\log_2 \frac{1}{q_i}} + \log_2 \frac{1}{c} \\ &= H(\pp{p}, \qq{q}) + \log_2 \frac1c \\ &= \hh{H(p)} + \underbrace{\ii{D(p\,\|\,q)}}_{\ge\, 0 \text{ (Gibbs)}} + \underbrace{\log_2 \frac1c}_{\ge\, 0 \text{ (Kraft)}}. \end{aligned} \]

So \(L \ge \hh{H}\). The formula also says exactly what a code loses and why: the violet term is the cost of shares that do not match the true probabilities, and the last term is the cost of unspent budget. Both vanish only if \(2^{-\ss{\ell_i}} = \pp{p_i}\) for every outcome, which needs every probability to be a power of \(\tfrac12\). The mountain town was such a case, and that is why its code hit the entropy exactly.

Part 2: within one bit

Use the Shannon code of section 11: \(\ss{\ell_i} = \lceil \log_2 (1/\pp{p_i}) \rceil\), the ideal length rounded up. Rounding up means \(\ss{\ell_i} \ge \log_2 (1/\pp{p_i})\), so \(2^{-\ss{\ell_i}} \le \pp{p_i}\), so the shares sum to at most \(\sum_i \pp{p_i} = 1\) and Kraft guarantees the code exists. Rounding up adds less than 1, so \(\ss{\ell_i} \lt \log_2 (1/\pp{p_i}) + 1\), and averaging:

\[ L = \sum_i \pp{p_i} \ss{\ell_i} \lt \sum_i \pp{p_i} \left( \log_2 \frac{1}{\pp{p_i}} + 1 \right) = \hh{H} + 1. \]

The Huffman code is at least as good as any other, so it too lies in the range \(\hh{H} \le L \lt \hh{H} + 1\).

Squeezing out the last bit

One bit of slack per symbol can be a lot (it was more than half the bill for the 90/10 source). Blocks remove it. Treat \(n\) independent reports as one super-symbol. By the additivity of section 8 its entropy is \(n \hh{H}\), so by the theorem there is a code for blocks with \(n \hh{H} \le L_n \lt n \hh{H} + 1\). Divide by \(n\):

\[ \hh{H} \;\le\; \frac{L_n}{n} \;\lt\; \hh{H} + \frac1n. \]

The cost per report is pinned between the entropy and the entropy plus \(\tfrac1n\). With long enough blocks you get as close to \(\hh{H}\) as you like, and never below. That is the precise sense in which entropy is the measure of information: it is the exchange rate between a random source and bits, exact in bulk.

Problem 13.1 · medium

A colleague announces a lossless compressor that makes every file of exactly 1000 bytes at least one bit shorter. Disprove it by counting, without mentioning probability at all. Then explain what the source coding theorem adds to the counting argument.

Solution

There are \(2^{8000}\) different files of 8000 bits. The number of bit strings shorter than 8000 bits is \(2^0 + 2^1 + \dots + 2^{7999} = 2^{8000} - 1\), one fewer. So two different input files must be mapped to the same output (the pigeonhole principle), and that output cannot be decompressed to both. No lossless compressor shrinks every input.

Counting only says that some files must grow or stay the same. The theorem says how the unavoidable trade should be made: give short outputs to probable inputs, at lengths near their surprise, and the average is the entropy. A compressor is a bet about which files are likely.

14Cross-entropy as a loss function

If you have trained a classifier or read about language models, you have met a "cross-entropy loss". It is the quantity of section 12, used for a different purpose. The station knew the truth \(\pp{p}\) and designed a code. A learning system does the reverse: it does not know \(\pp{p}\), it only sees samples from it, and it adjusts a model \(\qq{q}\) to make the cross-entropy small. By Gibbs' inequality the cross-entropy is smallest when \(\qq{q} = \pp{p}\), so pushing it down pulls the model towards the truth.

One example at a time

An image classifier looks at a photo and outputs a model distribution over labels, say cat \(\qq{0.7}\), dog \(\qq{0.2}\), bird \(\qq{0.1}\). The photo is in fact a dog. The loss charged for this example is the model's surprise at the truth:

\[ \text{loss} = \qq{\log_2 \frac{1}{q(\text{correct label})}} = \log_2 \frac{1}{0.2} = 2.32 \text{ bits.} \]

This is a cross-entropy in which the truth is the distribution that puts probability 1 on "dog": the sum \(\sum_i \pp{p_i} \log_2 (1/\qq{q_i})\) has a single non-zero term. Had the model said dog \(0.99\), the loss would be 0.014 bits; had it said \(0.001\), nearly 10 bits. The loss rewards putting probability on what actually happens and punishes confident mistakes without limit.

Averaged over a dataset

Average that loss over all training examples and you get the expected surprise of the model under the frequencies found in the data: the cross-entropy between the data and the model. Since \(H(p, q) = H(p) + D(p\,\|\,q)\) and the entropy of the data \(H(p)\) does not depend on the model, minimising cross-entropy is the same as minimising the KL divergence from the data to the model.

It is also the same as maximum likelihood, the oldest principle in statistics: choose the model under which the observed data is most probable. For independent examples the probability of the whole dataset is a product of per-example probabilities, and by the product rule for logarithms the total surprise is the sum of the per-example surprises. Making the product as large as possible is making the sum of surprises as small as possible. Three names, one procedure.

Units

Libraries use the natural logarithm, so their losses are in nats. A handy check: a classifier with 10 classes that has learned nothing outputs \(\tfrac1{10}\) for each, and its loss is \(\ln 10 \approx 2.303\) nats (\(\log_2 10 \approx 3.32\) bits). If a freshly initialised network reports a loss far from that, something is miswired.

Language models and compression

A language model outputs, at every position, a distribution \(\qq{q}\) over the next token. Its training loss is the average surprise at the token that actually came next: the cross-entropy between real text and the model, in bits (or nats) per token. People often quote it as perplexity, \(2^{H(p,q)}\) when \(H\) is in bits. A perplexity of 8 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 8 tokens.

And here the two halves of this page meet. Section 13 says a code built on model \(\qq{q}\) spends \(H(\pp{p}, \qq{q})\) bits per symbol. So a language model is a compressor: feed its predictions to an arithmetic coder and the text shrinks to its cross-entropy. A better predictor is a better compressor, and the other way round. Claude Shannon tested this on people in 1951, having them guess a text letter by letter, and estimated that English carries roughly 0.6 to 1.3 bits per letter, against \(\log_2 27 \approx 4.75\) for 26 letters and a space chosen uniformly. Most of what we write is, in the strict sense, predictable.

Problem 14.1 · easy

A spam filter says a message is spam with probability \(0.25\). It is spam. What is the loss in bits? What would the loss have been had the message been legitimate?

Solution

Spam: \(\log_2 (1/0.25) = 2\) bits.

Legitimate: the model gave that outcome \(1 - 0.25 = 0.75\), so the loss is \(\log_2 (1/0.75) = \log_2 4 - \log_2 3 = 2 - 1.585 = 0.415\) bits. The loss looks only at the probability assigned to what really happened.

Problem 14.2 · medium

A language model with a vocabulary of 50,000 tokens reaches a cross-entropy of 3 bits per token on some text. (a) What is its perplexity? (b) What would the cross-entropy and perplexity of a model that predicts uniformly be? Use \(\log_2 50{,}000 \approx 15.6\). (c) If the text is 1000 tokens long, to how many bits could an arithmetic coder driven by the model compress it?

(a) \(2^3 = 8\).

Problem 14.3 · harder

A model is trained on coin flips that are heads 70% of the time. It can only output a single number \(\qq{q}\), its probability of heads. Show that the cross-entropy is minimised at \(\qq{q} = 0.7\) by comparing \(\qq{q} = 0.6\), \(0.7\) and \(0.8\) numerically, and then say why no computation was needed.

Solution

The cross-entropy is \(0.7 \log_2 \tfrac1q + 0.3 \log_2 \tfrac1{1-q}\).

\(q = 0.6\): \(0.7 \cdot 0.737 + 0.3 \cdot 1.322 = 0.913\).

\(q = 0.7\): \(0.7 \cdot 0.515 + 0.3 \cdot 1.737 = 0.881\).

\(q = 0.8\): \(0.7 \cdot 0.322 + 0.3 \cdot 2.322 = 0.922\).

The minimum is at \(0.7\), where the cross-entropy equals the entropy of the coin, 0.881 bits. No computation was needed because Gibbs' inequality says \(H(p, q) \ge H(p)\) with equality only at \(q = p\). Notice that the best achievable loss is not zero: it is the entropy of the data. A loss that plateaus above zero may simply have reached the randomness that is really there.

Conditioning: zooming in on a row

You wake up and the sky is cloudy. What is the chance of rain now? Cloudy mornings make up \(\tfrac38\) of all days. Rainy afternoons after cloudy mornings make up \(\tfrac14\) of all days. So among cloudy mornings, the fraction that turn rainy is

\[ P(\text{rain} \mid \text{cloudy}) = \frac{P(\text{cloudy and rain})}{P(\text{cloudy})} = \frac{1/4}{3/8} = \frac23. \]

The bar \(\mid\) reads "given". Conditioning means: throw away every day that does not match what you observed, and rescale what is left so that it sums to 1 again. Before looking out of the window the chance of rain was \(\tfrac38\); a cloudy sky raises it to \(\tfrac23\), and a clear one lowers it to \(\tfrac{1/8}{5/8} = \tfrac15\).

Definitions

Conditional probability: \(P(y \mid x) = \dfrac{P(x, y)}{P(x)}\).

Product rule (the same equation, rearranged): \(P(x, y) = P(x) \cdot P(y \mid x)\). First \(x\) has to happen, then \(y\) given that \(x\) did.

Independence is the special case \(P(y \mid x) = P(y)\) for all \(x\): observing \(x\) changes nothing. Then the product rule becomes \(P(x, y) = P(x) \cdot P(y)\), the rule of section 3.

Bayes' rule: turning the question around

The afternoon brought rain. What is the chance that the morning had been cloudy? This is the reverse of the question before, and the table answers it the same way, zooming in on a column instead of a row: \(P(\text{cloudy} \mid \text{rain}) = \tfrac{1/4}{3/8} = \tfrac23\).

Often you do not have the table, only the conditionals in one direction. The product rule can be written in two orders, \(P(x, y) = P(x) P(y \mid x) = P(y) P(x \mid y)\). Setting the two right-hand sides equal and dividing by \(P(y)\) gives Bayes' rule:

\[ P(x \mid y) = \frac{P(y \mid x) \cdot P(x)}{P(y)}. \]

In the mosaic, \(P(\text{cloudy} \mid \text{rain})\) is the cloudy rain tile's area divided by the total area of both rain tiles.

Problem 15.2 · medium

A disease affects 1% of people. A test detects it in 90% of those who have it, and gives a false alarm for 5% of those who do not. Your test is positive. What is the probability that you have the disease?

Solution

Picture 10,000 people. 100 are ill, and 90 of them test positive. 9,900 are healthy, and 5% of them, 495, test positive as well. So there are \(90 + 495 = 585\) positive tests, of which 90 belong to ill people:

\[ P(\text{ill} \mid \text{positive}) = \frac{90}{585} \approx 0.154. \]

With Bayes' rule: \(\dfrac{0.9 \cdot 0.01}{0.9 \cdot 0.01 + 0.05 \cdot 0.99} = \dfrac{0.009}{0.0585}\).

16Two variables: mutual information

How much does the morning sky tell you about the afternoon? "Something, but not everything" is the honest answer from the table. Entropy lets us say exactly how much, in bits.

Three entropies from one table

Let \(X\) be the morning sky and \(Y\) the afternoon. There are three distributions in the table and each has an entropy.

The marginal entropies, from the totals. Both margins happen to be \((\tfrac58, \tfrac38)\):

\[ \hh{H}(X) = \hh{H}(Y) = \tfrac58 \log_2 \tfrac85 + \tfrac38 \log_2 \tfrac83 = 0.424 + 0.531 = 0.954 \text{ bits.} \]

The joint entropy, treating each pair as one outcome with the four inner probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac18, \tfrac18, \tfrac14\):

\[ \hh{H}(X, Y) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 = 1.75 \text{ bits.} \]

If sky and afternoon were independent, section 8 says the joint entropy would be the sum \(0.954 + 0.954 = 1.909\). It is only \(1.75\). Describing the pair costs \(\ii{0.159}\) bits less than describing the two halves separately, because part of what the second half says, the first half already told you.

Conditional entropy: the uncertainty that remains

Approach it from the other side. You have seen the sky; how uncertain is the afternoon now? That depends on what you saw.

  • After a clear morning the afternoon is \((\tfrac45 \text{ dry}, \tfrac15 \text{ rain})\), with entropy \(0.722\) bits.
  • After a cloudy morning it is \((\tfrac13 \text{ dry}, \tfrac23 \text{ rain})\), with entropy \(0.918\) bits.

Clear mornings occur \(\tfrac58\) of the time and cloudy ones \(\tfrac38\), so on an average day the remaining uncertainty is

\[ \hh{H}(Y \mid X) = \tfrac58 \cdot 0.722 + \tfrac38 \cdot 0.918 = 0.796 \text{ bits.} \]
Definition: conditional entropy

\(\hh{H}(Y \mid X) = \displaystyle\sum_x \pp{p(x)} \cdot \hh{H}(Y \mid X = x)\): the entropy of \(Y\) within each row, averaged over the rows with the rows' probabilities as weights. It is an average of an average.

The chain rule

The numbers so far satisfy \(0.954 + 0.796 = 1.75\), that is,

\[ \hh{H}(X, Y) = \hh{H}(X) + \hh{H}(Y \mid X). \]

In words: to describe the pair, first describe \(X\), then describe \(Y\) to someone who already knows \(X\). This always holds, and the proof is the product rule pushed through a logarithm. The surprise of a pair is

\[ \ss{\log_2 \frac{1}{p(x, y)}} = \log_2 \frac{1}{p(x) \, p(y \mid x)} = \ss{\log_2 \frac{1}{p(x)}} + \ss{\log_2 \frac{1}{p(y \mid x)}}, \]

the surprise at \(x\) plus the surprise at \(y\) once \(x\) is known. Now average over all pairs, weighting by \(p(x, y)\). The left side averages to \(\hh{H}(X, Y)\). The first term on the right depends only on \(x\), and averaging it gives \(\hh{H}(X)\). For the second term, write the weight as \(p(x, y) = p(x) \, p(y \mid x)\):

\[ \sum_x \sum_y p(x) \, p(y \mid x) \log_2 \frac{1}{p(y \mid x)} = \sum_x p(x) \underbrace{\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 \frac{1}{p(y \mid x)}}_{\hh{H}(Y \mid X = x)} = \hh{H}(Y \mid X). \]

Problem 8.2, where splitting an outcome of probability \(p\) in two added \(p\) bits, was this rule in miniature.

Mutual information

Before looking at the sky the afternoon had \(0.954\) bits of uncertainty. After looking, \(0.796\) remain on average. The difference is what the sky told you.

Definition: mutual information

The mutual information between \(X\) and \(Y\) is the average reduction in uncertainty about one from learning the other:

\[ \ii{I(X; Y)} = \hh{H}(Y) - \hh{H}(Y \mid X) = \hh{H}(X) - \hh{H}(X \mid Y) = \hh{H}(X) + \hh{H}(Y) - \hh{H}(X, Y). \]

The three forms are equal by the chain rule (substitute \(\hh{H}(Y \mid X) = \hh{H}(X,Y) - \hh{H}(X)\) into the first to get the third, which is symmetric in \(X\) and \(Y\)).

For the mountain town, \(\ii{I} = 0.954 - 0.796 = 0.159\) bits, the same overlap found from the joint entropy. The symmetry is worth a second look: the sky tells you exactly as much about the afternoon as the afternoon tells you about the sky. Information has no direction.

Information never hurts, on average

Mutual information is a KL divergence in disguise. Compare the true joint distribution \(p(x, y)\) with the model that pretends the variables are independent, \(p(x) \, p(y)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} D\bigl(p(x,y) \,\|\, p(x)p(y)\bigr) &= \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} \\ &= \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \left( \log_2 \frac{1}{p(x)} + \log_2 \frac{1}{p(y)} - \log_2 \frac{1}{p(x,y)} \right) \\ &= \hh{H}(X) + \hh{H}(Y) - \hh{H}(X,Y) = \ii{I(X;Y)}. \end{aligned} \]

So mutual information is the price, in bits per day, of coding the pair as if its halves were unrelated. That is why it shares the violet of section 12. And Gibbs' inequality applies: \(\ii{I(X;Y)} \ge 0\), with equality exactly when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. Rearranged, \(\hh{H}(Y \mid X) \le \hh{H}(Y)\): on average, learning something never increases your uncertainty about something else.

"On average" matters

A particular observation can leave you more uncertain. Say rain is rare, 10% overall, so \(H(Y) = 0.469\) bits, but on the uncommon days when dark clouds gather it is a coin flip. Seeing the dark clouds raises your uncertainty to 1 bit. The theorem survives because the many cloudless days, on which rain becomes nearly impossible, more than make up for it in the average.

Where this is used

Decision trees. A decision tree classifier picks, at each node, the feature to split on. The standard criterion, information gain, is the mutual information between the feature and the label: split on the feature whose value removes the most uncertainty about the answer.

Guessing games, again. The answer to a question is determined by the secret, so \(H(\text{answer} \mid \text{secret}) = 0\) and the information a question delivers is simply the entropy of its answer. A yes/no answer has at most 1 bit, reached when yes and no are equally likely. This is the principle from section 5, ask the question you are least able to predict the answer to, and it is how entropy-based Wordle solvers choose their guesses.

Communication over noise. If \(X\) is what you send and \(Y\) what arrives, \(I(X; Y)\) is how many bits per use actually get through. Its maximum over all ways of using the channel is the channel's capacity, the subject of the second half of Shannon's 1948 paper.

import math

type JointTable = dict[tuple[str, str], float]

    return sum(-p * math.log2(p) for p in probabilities if p > 0)

    return totals

Problem 16.1 · easy

Roll a fair die. Let \(X\) be the number shown and \(Y\) whether it is even or odd. Find \(H(X)\), \(H(Y)\), \(H(Y \mid X)\), \(I(X; Y)\) and \(H(X \mid Y)\).

Solution

\(H(X) = \log_2 6 = 2.585\) bits and \(H(Y) = 1\) bit (three even faces, three odd).

\(H(Y \mid X) = 0\): once you know the number, the parity is certain.

\(I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = 1\) bit. The number tells you everything about the parity.

\(H(X \mid Y) = H(X) - I(X; Y) = 1.585\) bits \(= \log_2 3\): knowing the parity leaves three equally likely faces. The information is symmetric (1 bit both ways) even though the remaining uncertainties are not (0 one way, 1.585 the other).

Problem 16.2 · medium

You have 8 emails, 4 spam and 4 legitimate. Of the 4 that contain the word "free", 3 are spam. Of the 4 that do not, 1 is spam. What is the information gain of splitting on "contains free"? Use \(H(\tfrac34, \tfrac14) = 0.811\).

Solution

Before the split the label is 4 against 4: \(H(\text{label}) = 1\) bit.

After the split, each branch is 3 against 1, with entropy 0.811 bits. Each branch holds half of the emails:

\[ H(\text{label} \mid \text{free}) = \tfrac12 \cdot 0.811 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.811 = 0.811. \]

Information gain \(= 1 - 0.811 = 0.189\) bits. The feature helps, but removes less than a fifth of the uncertainty. A feature that split the emails into pure groups would gain the full 1 bit; a feature unrelated to spam would gain 0.

Problem 16.3 · harder

The original Wordle has 2,315 possible answers. Each guess is answered with a colour pattern: five tiles, each grey, yellow or green. Using only counting, show that no strategy can guarantee a win in two guesses. How many bits is the answer worth, and what is the most one guess's feedback can deliver?

Solution

The answer is worth \(\log_2 2315 \approx 11.18\) bits. A pattern is one of \(3^5 = 243\) possibilities, so one guess's feedback delivers at most \(\log_2 243 \approx 7.92\) bits, and that only if all 243 patterns were equally likely.

To win on guess two, the second guess must be the answer, so the feedback from guess one alone must identify it. But one pattern sorts the 2,315 answers into at most 243 groups, so some group contains at least \(\lceil 2315 / 243 \rceil = 10\) words, and from inside that group you cannot always pick correctly. In bits: \(7.92 \lt 11.18\).

Two rounds of feedback give up to \(243^2 = 59{,}049\) combinations, more than 2,315, so counting does not rule out always knowing the answer by guess three. (Real solvers need a little more, because no guess splits the list evenly: the best openers deliver a bit under 6 bits, not 7.92.)

17Entropy in physics

A detour that came first

The word "entropy" is eighty years older than Shannon's paper. It comes from the physics of heat. This section shows that the physicists' entropy is the same quantity as the one built above, applied to a particular question: how much do we not know about the atoms?

Microstates and macrostates

Picture a closed box containing 100 gas particles, and imagine a line down its middle. A complete description of the gas, its microstate, would say for every single particle which half it is in. (A real microstate also has exact positions and speeds; left-or-right is enough to see the idea.) There are \(2^{100}\) microstates, and if the particles wander about freely, all are equally likely.

Nobody can see a microstate. What you can measure is something coarse, like the pressure on each side, which tells you only how many particles are in the left half. That is the macrostate. Many microstates look alike from outside: the macrostate "exactly 1 particle on the left" can be realised in 100 ways, one for each choice of the lonely particle. In general, "\(k\) on the left" is realised by \(W = \binom{100}{k}\) microstates, the number of ways of choosing which \(k\) particles they are.

import math


def binary_entropy_bits(probability: float) -> float:
    if probability in (0.0, 1.0):
        return 0.0
    return -probability * math.log2(probability) - (1 - probability) * math.log2(1 - probability)

Boltzmann's entropy

The entropy of a macrostate that can be realised by \(W\) equally likely microstates is

\[ S = k_B \ln W. \]

Since \(\ln W = \ln 2 \cdot \log_2 W\), this is \(S = (k_B \ln 2) \cdot \log_2 W\): the number of bits of missing information about the microstate, times a constant. Boltzmann's constant \(k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\) joules per kelvin is only a conversion factor between the units of information and the units in which entropy had been measured, long before, with thermometers.

Why entropy increases

Start the gas in the most special state there is, all particles in the left half, and let them move at random. In the demo, each tick picks one particle at random and moves it to the other side.

No rule in the simulation prefers the even split. Every microstate is as likely as every other. But the macrostates are wildly unequal in size. From the table, the 21 macrostates from 40 to 60 on the left hold 96.5% of all microstates, so a randomly wandering system is nearly always found there. The return trip to "all on the left" is not forbidden. It has probability \(2^{-100} \approx 8 \times 10^{-31}\), and that is for a mere hundred particles; a litre of air has about \(10^{22}\).

This is the second law of thermodynamics: the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. Seen this way it is not a law of force but of counting. A system moves from macrostates with few microstates to macrostates with many, for the same reason that a shuffled deck is not found in sorted order. Milk mixes into coffee and does not unmix, because "mixed" is almost every arrangement there is.

Shannon's formula, hiding in the counting

The last column of the table, N * H(k/N), is the binary entropy function of section 8 applied to the fraction of particles on the left, times the number of particles. It tracks \(\log_2 W\) closely. Here is why.

We need the size of a factorial. Stirling's approximation says \(\ln n! \approx n \ln n - n\) for large \(n\). (The reason in one line: \(\ln n! = \ln 1 + \ln 2 + \dots + \ln n\), a sum of \(n\) values of the logarithm, which is close to the area under the curve \(\ln x\) from 1 to \(n\), and that area is \(n \ln n - n + 1\).) Apply it to \(W = \dfrac{N!}{k! \, (N-k)!}\), whose logarithm is \(\ln N! - \ln k! - \ln (N-k)!\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln W &\approx \bigl(N \ln N - N\bigr) - \bigl(k \ln k - k\bigr) - \bigl((N-k) \ln (N-k) - (N-k)\bigr) \\ &= N \ln N - k \ln k - (N-k) \ln (N-k) && \text{the } {-N} + k + (N-k) \text{ cancel} \\ &= k \ln N + (N-k) \ln N - k \ln k - (N-k) \ln (N-k) && \text{split } N \ln N \\ &= -k \ln \frac{k}{N} - (N-k) \ln \frac{N-k}{N} && \text{pair up the terms} \\ &= N \left( -\frac{k}{N} \ln \frac{k}{N} - \frac{N-k}{N} \ln \frac{N-k}{N} \right). \end{aligned} \]

Write \(f = k/N\) for the fraction on the left and divide by \(\ln 2\) to change to bits:

\[ \log_2 W \approx N \cdot \bigl( f \log_2 \tfrac1f + (1-f) \log_2 \tfrac{1}{1-f} \bigr) = N \cdot \hh{H}(f). \]

So Shannon's formula is what counting arrangements turns into when the numbers are large. Each particle behaves like a coin that lands "left" with probability \(f\), carrying \(\hh{H}(f)\) bits, and \(N\) particles carry \(N\) times as much. Boltzmann's entropy is the entropy of section 7, summed over the particles. (The table's two columns differ by a few bits out of a hundred. That gap comes from the crude form of Stirling's formula and grows only like \(\log N\), so for \(10^{22}\) particles it is utterly negligible.)

When the microstates are not equally likely, the general formula is Gibbs's, \(S = -k_B \sum_i p_i \ln p_i\), summed over microstates. It is Shannon's \(H\) with natural logarithms and a factor of \(k_B\), and it reduces to Boltzmann's when all \(W\) microstates have \(p_i = 1/W\).

Information is physical

If thermodynamic entropy is missing information, then handling information should have thermodynamic consequences. It does. Landauer's principle (1961) says that erasing one bit, forcing a memory cell that could be 0 or 1 into a known state, must release at least \(k_B T \ln 2\) of heat into surroundings at temperature \(T\). At room temperature that is about \(2.9 \times 10^{-21}\) joules: the entropy that leaves the memory has to go somewhere. The bound was confirmed experimentally in 2012, and it is what finally settled the century-old puzzle of Maxwell's demon, an imagined being that sorts fast from slow molecules and seems to lower entropy for free. The demon must record what it sees, and wiping that record costs at least as much entropy as the sorting gained.

Solution

Problem 17.2 · medium

18Pitfalls

Entropy belongs to a distribution, not to a string

"What is the entropy of hunter2?" has no answer. Entropy is a property of the process that produces outcomes, of the probabilities, and a single outcome has only a surprise, relative to some distribution. The same eight letters could come from a uniform random generator (37.6 bits) or from a person who always uses that password (0 bits). Password-strength meters that print "entropy" are quietly assuming a generating process.

Entropy depends on the model you measure with

A very common computation: count how often each byte occurs in a file, plug the frequencies into the formula, and call the result "the entropy of the file". What that measures is the entropy of the source "draw each byte independently with these frequencies". It ignores all structure between bytes, and can be far from what a compressor achieves, in either direction:

import math
import random
import zlib
from collections import Counter


def letter_frequency_entropy_bits(data: bytes) -> float:
    """Entropy of the distribution 'pick one byte of `data` at random'."""
    byte_counts = Counter(data)
    return sum(
        -(count / len(data)) * math.log2(count / len(data)) for count in byte_counts.values()
    )


random_generator = random.Random(2026)
alternating = b"ab" * 50_000
shuffled = bytes(random_generator.choice(b"ab") for _ in range(100_000))
uniform_noise = random_generator.randbytes(100_000)

    print(

And uniformly random bytes cannot be compressed at all; zlib's output is slightly longer than its input, exactly as problem 13.1 says must sometimes happen.

Bits and nats

\(\log_2\) gives bits, \(\ln\) gives nats, and 1 nat is about 1.443 bits. Papers and libraries rarely say which they mean. If a reported cross-entropy looks off by a factor of about 0.69 or 1.44, this is why.

Zeros

In an entropy, \(0 \log 0 = 0\) and zeros are skipped. In a cross-entropy, a zero in the model at an outcome that occurs gives an infinite loss, and in floating point, a nan or inf that poisons the whole average. Practical code never lets a model output an exact zero: it works with logarithms of probabilities directly, or clips, or smooths.

KL divergence is not a distance

\(D(p\,\|\,q) \ne D(q\,\|\,p)\) in general, and there is no triangle inequality. Always say which argument is the truth and which is the model.

Estimating entropy from samples is biased downwards

Take 10 samples from a uniform distribution over 1000 values. Almost certainly all 10 are different, so the observed frequencies are \(\tfrac1{10}\) each and the plug-in estimate is \(\log_2 10 = 3.3\) bits. The true entropy is \(\log_2 1000 = 10\) bits. With too few samples you have not seen most of the outcomes, and what you have not seen you cannot be surprised by. Entropy estimates from small samples, and from high-order n-gram statistics, are too low.

"Within one bit" can be a lot

\(H \le L \lt H + 1\) is per symbol. For a source with \(H = 0.08\) bits (a 99/1 coin) a symbol code is twelve times worse than the entropy. The fix is to code blocks, not to distrust the theorem.

"On average" is part of every statement

Conditioning reduces entropy on average, not for each observation. Codes are short on average, not for each message. Entropy increases with overwhelming probability, not with certainty; the gas demo's curve visibly dips.

"Entropy is disorder"

The slogan is a loose paraphrase at best. Entropy counts the microstates compatible with what you know. A shuffled deck is not a higher-entropy object than a sorted one; each is a single arrangement. What has high entropy is the macrostate "some arrangement I would call shuffled", because nearly all arrangements qualify.

Continuous quantities

For a continuous variable such as a real-valued measurement, the analogous integral, the differential entropy, behaves differently: it can be negative and it changes when you change units. Specifying a real number exactly would take infinitely many bits. Differences of differential entropies, and so KL divergence and mutual information, remain meaningful. Everything on this page concerns the discrete case.

  3. Entropy runs from 0 to \(\log_2 n\). Zero when the outcome is certain, largest when all \(n\) outcomes are equally likely.
  4. A wrong model costs exactly \(D(p\,\|\,q)\) extra bits, never a negative amount. Training a model by cross-entropy is searching for the belief that wastes the fewest bits; predicting well and compressing well are the same skill.

20Quiz

A mixed review. Try each before opening the solution.

Quiz 1 · easy

In the game "twenty questions", how many different objects can a perfect questioner tell apart? What does that say about how the questions should be chosen?

Solution

\(2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576\), about a million. To reach that, every question must split the remaining candidates into two equally likely halves, so that each answer delivers a full bit. "Is it a giraffe?" as a first question delivers a tiny fraction of a bit: the answer is almost certainly no.

Quiz 2 · easy

Without computing, order these from lowest to highest entropy: a fair die; a fair coin; a server that is up 97% of the time and in one of three failure modes 1% each; a clock that always shows the correct time.

Solution

Clock (0 bits: no uncertainty) < server (0.24 bits: nearly certain) < coin (1 bit) < die (2.585 bits).

The trap is the server. It has four outcomes, more than the coin's two, but entropy does not count outcomes, it weighs them. Four outcomes can hold up to 2 bits, and this distribution uses very little of that.

Quiz 3 · medium

A code uses lengths 1, 2, 3, 3 for four symbols. (a) For which distribution is it perfect, with \(L = H\)? (b) What is its average length if the four symbols are in fact equally likely, and how many bits per symbol are wasted?

Solution

(a) The one where each probability equals its share of the budget, \(p_i = 2^{-\ell_i}\): \((\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)\).

(b) \(L = \tfrac14 (1 + 2 + 3 + 3) = 2.25\) bits. The entropy of the uniform distribution on four symbols is 2 bits, so 0.25 bits are wasted. That waste is \(D(\text{uniform} \,\|\, (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18))\), and by coincidence it equals the divergence in the opposite direction found in problem 12.1.

Quiz 4 · medium

Twelve coins, one of which is fake and either heavier or lighter than the rest, you do not know which. With a balance scale, you must find the fake and say whether it is heavy or light. Show that two weighings cannot be enough. Does the same argument rule out three?

Solution

There are \(12 \cdot 2 = 24\) possibilities (which coin, and heavy or light): \(\log_2 24 \approx 4.58\) bits. One weighing has three results, at most \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\) bits.

Two weighings: \(3^2 = 9\) result strings, fewer than 24. Impossible. (\(3.17 \lt 4.58\) bits.)

Three weighings: \(3^3 = 27 \ge 24\), or \(4.75 \ge 4.58\) bits. The counting argument does not rule it out, and in fact a three-weighing solution exists, though finding it is a famous puzzle. Counting gives lower bounds; it never promises that the bound can be reached.

Quiz 5 · medium

You compress a large text file with a good compressor and get a file a quarter of the size. You compress the result again with the same program. What happens, and why?

Solution

Almost nothing; the file usually grows by a few bytes. A good compressor has already removed the redundancy, so its output is close to its own entropy: the bits look like fair coin flips, each carrying nearly a full bit. A source that is already uniform has maximal entropy, and section 13 says nothing can go below that. If the second pass did shrink the file noticeably, it would be evidence that the first pass was poor.

Quiz 6 · medium

A classifier's average cross-entropy loss on its training set reaches exactly 0. What does that tell you about its outputs? Is this always achievable?

Solution

Every term \(\log_2 (1 / q(\text{correct}))\) is non-negative, so an average of 0 forces every term to be 0: the model assigns probability 1 to the correct label of every training example.

It is achievable only if the labels are a function of the inputs. If two identical inputs carry different labels, no model can give probability 1 to both, and the floor is the conditional entropy \(H(\text{label} \mid \text{input})\), which is then above zero. And a model at zero training loss is maximally confident, which makes any mistake on new data infinitely costly.

Quiz 7 · medium

You are told that \(I(X; Y) = H(X)\). What is \(H(X \mid Y)\), and what does that mean? Can you conclude that \(Y\) is determined by \(X\)?

Solution

\(H(X \mid Y) = H(X) - I(X; Y) = 0\): once \(Y\) is known, nothing about \(X\) remains uncertain, so \(X\) is a function of \(Y\).

The converse does not follow. With \(Y\) a die roll and \(X\) its parity (problem 16.1), \(I = H(X) = 1\) bit, yet knowing the parity leaves \(Y\) quite uncertain. \(Y\) may carry more than it shares.

Quiz 8 · harder

Show that for any deterministic function \(f\), \(H(f(X)) \le H(X)\): processing data cannot create information. When does equality hold?

Hint

Expand \(H(X, f(X))\) with the chain rule in both orders.

Solution

Write \(Y = f(X)\). The chain rule in one order: \(H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = H(X) + 0\), since \(Y\) is determined by \(X\).

In the other order: \(H(X, Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y)\).

Setting them equal, \(H(Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)\), because a conditional entropy is an average of entropies and so never negative. Equality holds when \(H(X \mid Y) = 0\), that is, when \(X\) can be recovered from \(f(X)\): when \(f\) is one-to-one on the outcomes that actually occur. Lossless compression is such an \(f\); hashing, rounding and summarising are not.

Quiz 9 · harder

In the gas demo with 40 particles the entropy curve sometimes goes down. Does that contradict the second law? What would the curve look like with \(10^{22}\) particles?

Solution

It does not. The second law is statistical: a decrease is not impossible, only improbable, and how improbable depends on the size of the system. With 40 particles the typical fluctuation in the left-hand count is a few particles (about \(\sqrt{40}/2 \approx 3\)), which is a visible fraction of 40.

With \(N\) particles the fluctuations grow like \(\sqrt N\) while the total grows like \(N\), so the relative wobble shrinks like \(1/\sqrt N\). For \(10^{22}\) particles that is one part in \(10^{11}\). The curve would rise smoothly to its maximum and then look perfectly flat, which is the form in which the law was discovered.

22History

1865. Rudolf Clausius, studying steam engines, defines a quantity that never decreases in an isolated system and names it entropy, from the Greek for "transformation", choosing the word to resemble "energy". At this point it is a matter of heat and temperature, with no probabilities in sight.

1877. Ludwig Boltzmann explains Clausius's quantity by counting: entropy is proportional to the logarithm of the number of microscopic arrangements. The formula \(S = k \log W\), put into that form by Max Planck around 1900, is carved on Boltzmann's gravestone in Vienna. J. Willard Gibbs generalises it to unequal probabilities, giving \(-k \sum p_i \ln p_i\).

1928. Ralph Hartley, at Bell Labs, proposes measuring the information in a message by the logarithm of the number of messages that could have been sent: section 4's \(\log_2 N\).

1948. Claude Shannon publishes A Mathematical Theory of Communication. It introduces the entropy of a source with unequal probabilities, proves the source coding theorem, and then proves something nobody expected, that reliable communication over a noisy channel is possible at any rate below the channel's capacity. The paper also popularised the word bit, which Shannon credits to John Tukey. A widely repeated story, told by Myron Tribus, has John von Neumann advising Shannon to call his quantity "entropy", partly because the same formula was already in use in statistical mechanics, and partly because nobody really knows what entropy is, so in a debate Shannon would always have the advantage. The story may be embellished, but the first half of the advice was sound.

1949–1956. Leon Kraft proves the budget inequality for prefix codes in his master's thesis; Brockway McMillan extends it to all uniquely decodable codes.

1951. Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler introduce the divergence that carries their names. In the same year David Huffman, a student in Robert Fano's information theory class at MIT, is offered a term paper in place of the final exam: find the optimal binary code. He nearly gives up before thinking of building the tree from the leaves, beating the top-down method of his own professor. The result is published in 1952.

1957. Edwin Jaynes argues that statistical mechanics can be derived from Shannon's entropy and not the other way round: thermodynamic entropy is the uncertainty of an observer who knows only the macroscopic quantities.

1961–1982. Rolf Landauer shows that erasing information has an unavoidable thermodynamic cost, and Charles Bennett uses that to resolve Maxwell's demon. The Landauer bound is measured in the laboratory in 2012.
