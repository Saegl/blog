01A receipt that knows about angles

You already know the dot product. You have computed it every time you checked a shopping receipt.

itemquantityprice eachline total
apples30.501.50
bread22.254.50
milk41.204.80
total10.80

There are two lists here, the quantities \((\ca{3}, \ca{2}, \ca{4})\) and the prices \((\cb{0.50}, \cb{2.25}, \cb{1.20})\). To get the total you multiply the entries that sit in the same row and add up the results:

\[ \ca{3}\cdot\cb{0.50} + \ca{2}\cdot\cb{2.25} + \ca{4}\cdot\cb{1.20} = 10.80 \]

That operation, multiply matching entries, then add, is the dot product of the two lists. In code it is one line.

def dot(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    """Multiply matching entries, then add everything up."""
    if len(left) != len(right):
        raise ValueError(f"length mismatch: {len(left)} vs {len(right)}")
    return sum(left_entry * right_entry for left_entry, right_entry in zip(left, right))


item_names: list[str] = ["apples", "bread", "milk"]
quantities: list[float] = [3, 2, 4]
prices: list[float] = [0.50, 2.25, 1.20]

for name, quantity, price in zip(item_names, quantities, prices):
    print(f"{name:<7} {quantity:>2} x {price:>5.2f} = {quantity * price:>5.2f}")
print(f"{'total':<7} {dot(quantities, prices):>18.2f}")
apples   3 x  0.50 =  1.50
bread    2 x  2.25 =  4.50
milk     4 x  1.20 =  4.80
total                10.80

If that were all, the dot product would be a piece of bookkeeping and this page would end here. What makes it worth a long page is a fact that looks like a coincidence the first time you meet it.

Take two short lists, \((\ca{3}, \ca{1})\) and \((\cb{2}, \cb{4})\), and run the receipt computation on them:

\[ \ca{3}\cdot\cb{2} + \ca{1}\cdot\cb{4} = 6 + 4 = 10. \]

Now forget the arithmetic and draw the two lists as arrows on graph paper: one going 3 right and 1 up, the other going 2 right and 4 up. Measure them with a ruler and a protractor. The first arrow is \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.162\) long, the second is \(\sqrt{20} \approx 4.472\) long, and the angle between them is exactly \(45^\circ\). Multiply the two lengths together and then by the cosine of the angle (a number between \(-1\) and \(1\) that we will build from scratch in section 3; for \(45^\circ\) it is about \(0.7071\)):

\[ 3.162 \times 4.472 \times 0.7071 = 10.00. \]

The same ten. The receipt computation never looked at the picture. It has no idea what a length or an angle is; it multiplied four numbers and added. And yet it produced exactly the number you get from the ruler and the protractor.

The claim of this page

For any two arrows \(\va\) and \(\vb\), with \(\th\) the angle between them,

\[ \underbrace{\ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2} + \dots + \ca{a_n}\cb{b_n}}_{\text{arithmetic on the numbers}} \;=\; \underbrace{\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th}_{\text{measurements on the picture}} \]

Both sides are called the dot product, written \(\va\cdot\vb\).

This equality is why the dot product is everywhere. The left side is trivially cheap for a computer: a loop of multiply-and-add. The right side is what you actually want to know in a hundred situations: is the enemy in front of the guard or behind, how bright is this surface under that lamp, are these two documents about the same thing, how strongly does this input excite that neuron. The dot product lets you ask a geometric question and answer it with a for loop.

The plan is bottom up. We fix notation for vectors, then build the one piece of trigonometry the whole story rests on (cosine, plus one theorem about triangles). With that in hand we will not be handed the formula; we will go looking for a way to measure how much two arrows agree, and the receipt computation will fall out of the search. After that come the consequences: projection, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and then uses in graphics, machine learning, linear algebra, and finally spaces where the "arrows" are whole functions.

02Vectors, on one page

You said vectors are solid ground, so this section only fixes the words and symbols used below. Nothing here is new.

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, \(\vv{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)\). The numbers are its components and \(n\) is its dimension. With \(n = 2\) or \(3\) you can draw it as an arrow from the origin to the point with those coordinates. With \(n = 12\) (the word counts of a document) or \(n = 768\) (an embedding) you cannot draw it, but every formula on this page still works, and that is the point of having formulas.

  • Addition is component by component: \((1, 4) + (5, 1) = (6, 5)\). In the picture, put the tail of the second arrow at the tip of the first.
  • Scaling multiplies every component by the same number: \(3 \cdot (2, -1) = (6, -3)\). The arrow gets three times longer; a negative factor also flips it around.
  • Length comes from Pythagoras: \(\norm{\vv{v}} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2}\). So \(\norm{(3, 4)} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\). The double bars are read "the length of" or "the norm of".
  • A unit vector has length 1. Dividing any nonzero vector by its own length gives the unit vector pointing the same way, written with a hat: \(\hat{\vv{v}} = \vv{v} / \norm{\vv{v}}\). For \((3, 4)\) that is \((0.6, 0.8)\). This is called normalising.
  • The standard basis vectors are the unit arrows along the axes: \(\vv{e}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\vv{e}_2 = (0, 1)\) in two dimensions. Every vector is a mix of them: \((3, 1) = 3\,\vv{e}_1 + 1\,\vv{e}_2\).

Two vectors get fixed colours for the whole page: a is sky blue and b is chalk yellow. The angle θ between them is green. Anything that is a shadow of one arrow on another is coral, and what is left over, at right angles to the shadow, is lilac.

Warm-up easy

Let \(\vv{v} = (6, -8)\). Find \(\norm{\vv{v}}\), the unit vector \(\hat{\vv{v}}\), and \(\vv{v} + 2\,\vv{e}_2\).

Solution

\(\norm{\vv{v}} = \sqrt{6^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\). The square removes the minus sign; a common slip is to write \(36 - 64\).

\(\hat{\vv{v}} = (6/10, -8/10) = (0.6, -0.8)\). Check: \(0.36 + 0.64 = 1\).

\(\vv{v} + 2\,\vv{e}_2 = (6, -8) + (0, 2) = (6, -6)\).

03Detour: cosine from the ground up

Why this detour

The geometric half of the dot product is \(\norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th\). If cosine is a button on a calculator, that formula is a black box and so is everything built on it. Cosine is in fact a very plain thing, a single coordinate of a single point, and once you see it that way the dot product stops being mysterious. This section and the next build exactly the trigonometry the rest of the page uses and nothing more: cosine, sine as its sidekick, three symmetries, and one theorem about triangles.

Measuring a turn: degrees and radians

An angle measures how much you turn. The everyday unit is the degree: a full turn is \(360^\circ\), a half turn is \(180^\circ\), a quarter turn (a right angle) is \(90^\circ\). The number 360 is a historical convenience; it has many divisors and nothing else going for it.

Mathematics and every programming language use a different unit, the radian, and its definition is worth knowing because it is not arbitrary. Draw a circle of radius 1. Stand at the point \((1, 0)\) and walk counter-clockwise along the circle. The angle you have turned through, measured in radians, is the distance you have walked.

The full circumference of a circle of radius 1 is \(2\pi \approx 6.283\). So a full turn is \(2\pi\) radians, a half turn is \(\pi \approx 3.142\) radians, and a quarter turn is \(\pi/2 \approx 1.571\) radians. To convert, use the fact that \(180^\circ\) and \(\pi\) radians are the same turn:

\[ \text{radians} = \text{degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180}, \qquad \text{degrees} = \text{radians} \times \frac{180}{\pi}. \]

One radian is therefore \(180/\pi \approx 57.3^\circ\). In Python, math.cos and math.acos speak radians, and math.radians / math.degrees convert. This page writes angles in degrees in the prose because \(60^\circ\) is easier to picture than \(1.047\), and converts in code.

Cosine is an x-coordinate

Keep the circle of radius 1 centred at the origin. It is called the unit circle. Start at \((1, 0)\), turn counter-clockwise by an angle \(\th\), and look at where you are standing.

Definition

The point on the unit circle at angle \(\th\) has coordinates \((\cp{\cos\th},\ \cs{\sin\th})\).

That is the definition of both functions. Cosine is how far right of the centre the point is, and sine is how far up.

Nothing more is going on. Cosine is a function that takes a turn and returns an x-coordinate. Drag the white point around the circle below, or use the slider, and watch the coral segment. Its signed length is \(\cos\th\).

A few things can be read straight off the picture, with no formula needed.

  • At \(\th = 0^\circ\) the point is \((1, 0)\), so \(\cos 0^\circ = 1\) and \(\sin 0^\circ = 0\).
  • At \(\th = 90^\circ\) the point is \((0, 1)\), straight up, so \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\) and \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\).
  • At \(\th = 180^\circ\) the point is \((-1, 0)\), so \(\cos 180^\circ = -1\).
  • For angles between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) the point is in the right half, so cosine is positive. Between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) the point is in the left half, so cosine is negative.
  • The point is always on a circle of radius 1, so neither coordinate can exceed 1 in size: \(-1 \le \cos\th \le 1\).

That sign change at \(90^\circ\) is the single most useful fact about cosine for this page. It is what will let the dot product tell "pointing roughly the same way" from "pointing roughly opposite ways".

One identity for free

The point \((\cos\th, \sin\th)\) is at distance 1 from the origin. Distance comes from Pythagoras, so

\[ \cp{\cos^2\th} + \cs{\sin^2\th} = 1 \]

for every angle. (The notation \(\cos^2\th\) means \((\cos\th)^2\).) The readout under the demo shows this sum; it stays at 1.000 wherever you drag. This is the only trigonometric identity we will need, and it is nothing but the statement "the point is on the circle".

Three values worth knowing

Besides \(0^\circ\), \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\), three angles come up constantly, and each cosine can be worked out with Pythagoras alone.

45°. Standing at \(45^\circ\) on the unit circle, you are equally far right and up, so \(\cos 45^\circ = \sin 45^\circ = s\) for some number \(s\). The identity says \(s^2 + s^2 = 1\), so \(s^2 = \tfrac12\) and \(s = \sqrt{1/2} = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} \approx 0.7071\).

60°. An equilateral triangle has three \(60^\circ\) angles. Cut one with side 1 down the middle: the cut lands at the midpoint of the base, so the piece along the base is \(\tfrac12\). That piece is the horizontal reach of a unit-length side tilted at \(60^\circ\), which is \(\cos 60^\circ\). So \(\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12\). The height follows from the identity: \(h^2 = 1 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\), so \(\sin 60^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 0.8660\).

30°. The same triangle seen from its top corner: now the long leg is the adjacent one, so \(\cos 30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12\).

Three symmetries

Each of these is a reflection of the unit circle, and each can be checked by dragging the point in the demo above.

  • Turning the other way. Turning by \(-\th\) (clockwise) lands on the mirror image across the x-axis: same distance right, opposite height. So \(\cos(-\th) = \cos\th\). Cosine does not care which way round you measure an angle. This is why "the angle between two arrows" needs no direction.
  • Reflecting left to right. The angle \(180^\circ - \th\) lands on the mirror image across the y-axis: same height, opposite x. So \(\cos(180^\circ - \th) = -\cos\th\). This gives obtuse angles for free: \(\cos 120^\circ = -\cos 60^\circ = -\tfrac12\).
  • Swapping the axes. The angle \(90^\circ - \th\) lands on the mirror image across the diagonal line \(y = x\), and that reflection swaps the two coordinates. So \(\cos(90^\circ - \th) = \sin\th\). Sine is cosine measured from the other axis.
Problem 3.1 easy

Convert \(150^\circ\) to radians and \(\pi/4\) radians to degrees.

Solution

\(150 \times \pi/180 = 5\pi/6 \approx 2.618\) radians. Sanity check: it is a bit less than a half turn, and \(2.618\) is a bit less than \(\pi \approx 3.142\).

\(\pi/4 \times 180/\pi = 45^\circ\).

Problem 3.2 easy

Without computing anything, say whether each is positive, negative or zero: \(\cos 100^\circ\), \(\cos 300^\circ\), \(\cos 270^\circ\), \(\cos 179^\circ\).

Solution

Ask where the point is on the unit circle and whether it is right or left of centre.

\(100^\circ\): just past straight up, slightly left. Negative (and small: about \(-0.17\)).

\(300^\circ\): the same as \(-60^\circ\), lower right. Positive; by the first symmetry it equals \(\cos 60^\circ = 0.5\).

\(270^\circ\): straight down, the point \((0, -1)\). Zero.

\(179^\circ\): almost at \((-1, 0)\). Negative, and nearly \(-1\).

Problem 3.3 medium

Find \(\cos 135^\circ\) and \(\cos 150^\circ\) exactly, using the table and a symmetry.

Solution

Use \(\cos(180^\circ - \th) = -\cos\th\).

\(135^\circ = 180^\circ - 45^\circ\), so \(\cos 135^\circ = -\cos 45^\circ = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} \approx -0.707\).

\(150^\circ = 180^\circ - 30^\circ\), so \(\cos 150^\circ = -\cos 30^\circ = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx -0.866\).

The common wrong answer forgets the minus sign. Check against the picture: both angles are past \(90^\circ\), so the point is in the left half and the cosine must be negative.

Problem 3.4 medium

An angle \(\th\) between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) has \(\cos\th = -0.6\). What is \(\sin\th\)?

Hint

The point is on the unit circle. Which half of the circle are angles from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) in: upper or lower?

Solution

From \(\cos^2\th + \sin^2\th = 1\): \(\sin^2\th = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64\), so \(\sin\th = \pm 0.8\). The identity alone cannot pick the sign. Angles from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) are the upper half of the circle, where the height is positive, so \(\sin\th = 0.8\). The point is \((-0.6, 0.8)\).

Bigger circles: cosine as a shadow

The unit circle has radius 1. Blow the whole picture up by a factor \(L\) and every coordinate is multiplied by \(L\). So the point at angle \(\th\) on a circle of radius \(L\) is \((L\cos\th,\ L\sin\th)\). Read that sentence as a statement about arrows:

Key fact

An arrow of length \(\ca{L}\) tilted at angle \(\th\) from the x-axis has components

\[ (\ \cp{L\cos\th},\ \ \cs{L\sin\th}\ ). \]

Its horizontal reach is \(\cp{L\cos\th}\). Equivalently: \(\cos\th\) is the fraction of an arrow's length that survives when you look only at its horizontal part.

This is the school definition "cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse" with the triangle put back into its natural habitat. The arrow is the hypotenuse, its horizontal reach is the adjacent side, and \(\text{adjacent} = \text{hypotenuse} \times \cos\th\).

A good mental image is a shadow. Lean a stick of length \(L\) at angle \(\th\) to the ground, with the sun directly overhead. The stick's shadow on the ground has length \(L\cos\th\). A stick lying flat (\(\th = 0^\circ\)) has a shadow as long as itself. A stick standing straight up (\(\th = 90^\circ\)) has no shadow at all. And a stick leaning backwards, past vertical, casts its shadow on the other side of its foot; we count that as a negative shadow, and cosine going negative past \(90^\circ\) handles the bookkeeping automatically.

Hold on to the shadow picture. In section 5 the ground will be replaced by a second arrow, and the shadow of one arrow on another is what the dot product is made of.

Going backwards: arccos

Cosine takes an angle and gives a number in \([-1, 1]\). Sometimes you have the number and want the angle. The inverse function is called arccosine, written \(\arccos\) or \(\cos^{-1}\), and math.acos in Python.

There is a wrinkle: many angles share a cosine (\(60^\circ\), \(-60^\circ\), \(300^\circ\), \(420^\circ\) all give \(0.5\)), so "the" angle is ambiguous. By convention \(\arccos\) returns the one between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\). Look at the cosine curve in the first demo: from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) it slides from \(1\) down to \(-1\) without ever turning back, so on that stretch every value is hit exactly once. That range is exactly right for "the angle between two arrows", which is never more than a half turn.

Problem 3.5 easy

A 10 m ladder leans against a wall, making a \(60^\circ\) angle with the ground. How far is its foot from the wall? Then, as an arrow: what are the components of an arrow of length 10 at \(120^\circ\) from the x-axis?

Solution

The distance from foot to wall is the ladder's horizontal reach: \(10 \cos 60^\circ = 10 \times \tfrac12 = 5\) m.

For \(120^\circ\): \(\cos 120^\circ = -\cos 60^\circ = -\tfrac12\) and \(\sin 120^\circ = \sin 60^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) (reflecting across the y-axis keeps the height). Components: \((10 \times -\tfrac12,\ 10 \times 0.866) = (-5,\ 8.66)\). The arrow leans back over the origin, and its "shadow" is \(-5\).

Problem 3.6 medium

What are \(\arccos(0)\), \(\arccos(-1)\) and \(\arccos(0.5)\), in degrees? And why does math.acos(1.0000000000000002) raise ValueError: math domain error?

Solution

Ask "which angle from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) has this x-coordinate?". \(\arccos(0) = 90^\circ\), \(\arccos(-1) = 180^\circ\), \(\arccos(0.5) = 60^\circ\).

No point on the unit circle has an x-coordinate above 1, so no angle has that cosine and the function is undefined there. The value \(1.0000000000000002\) is the smallest double above 1, and it turns up in practice when floating-point rounding nudges a computed cosine of two parallel vectors just past 1. The fix is to clamp into \([-1, 1]\) before calling acos; you will see that line in the code in section 9.

04Detour: the law of cosines

Why this detour

Pythagoras relates the three sides of a triangle when one angle is \(90^\circ\). We will need the version that works for any angle, because the triangle formed by two arbitrary arrows usually has no right angle. That version is the law of cosines. It is used once, in section 7, to give an independent proof of the page's main claim.

Take a triangle with two sides of lengths \(\ca{p}\) and \(\cb{q}\) meeting at an angle \(\ct{\gamma}\), and ask for the length \(r\) of the third side, the one facing that angle.

If \(\ct{\gamma} = 90^\circ\), Pythagoras gives \(r^2 = \ca{p}^2 + \cb{q}^2\). Now imagine the two sides are joined by a hinge. Close the hinge and the far ends approach each other: \(r\) shrinks below what Pythagoras says. Open the hinge past \(90^\circ\) and the far ends move apart: \(r\) grows beyond it. So the true formula must be Pythagoras plus a correction that is negative for small angles, zero at \(90^\circ\), and positive for large ones. Cosine has exactly the opposite sign pattern, which is a strong hint about what the correction looks like.

Deriving it

Put the hinge \(C\) at the origin and lay side \(\cb{q}\) along the x-axis, so its far end is \(A = (\cb{q}, 0)\). Side \(\ca{p}\) is an arrow of length \(\ca{p}\) at angle \(\ct{\gamma}\), and by the key fact of the last section its far end is \(B = (\ca{p}\cos\ct{\gamma},\ \ca{p}\sin\ct{\gamma})\). The third side runs from \(A\) to \(B\), and the distance formula (Pythagoras again) gives its squared length:

\[ r^2 = (\ca{p}\cos\ct{\gamma} - \cb{q})^2 + (\ca{p}\sin\ct{\gamma} - 0)^2. \]

Expand the first square using \((x - y)^2 = x^2 - 2xy + y^2\):

\[ r^2 = \ca{p}^2\cos^2\ct{\gamma} - 2\ca{p}\cb{q}\cos\ct{\gamma} + \cb{q}^2 + \ca{p}^2\sin^2\ct{\gamma}. \]

Group the two terms that contain \(\ca{p}^2\):

\[ r^2 = \ca{p}^2\left(\cos^2\ct{\gamma} + \sin^2\ct{\gamma}\right) + \cb{q}^2 - 2\ca{p}\cb{q}\cos\ct{\gamma}. \]

The bracket is the one identity we have, and it equals 1.

Law of cosines \[ r^2 = \ca{p}^2 + \cb{q}^2 - 2\,\ca{p}\,\cb{q}\cos\ct{\gamma} \]

Here \(\ca{p}\) and \(\cb{q}\) are two sides of a triangle, \(\ct{\gamma}\) is the angle between them, and \(r\) is the side facing that angle.

Because the derivation used the unit-circle definition of cosine and not a picture of an acute triangle, it holds for every \(\ct{\gamma}\) from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) with no separate cases. And the correction term \(-2\ca{p}\cb{q}\cos\ct{\gamma}\) behaves as the hinge argument predicted: negative below \(90^\circ\), zero at \(90^\circ\), positive above.

Problem 4.1 easy

Two sides of a triangle are 5 and 8, with a \(60^\circ\) angle between them. How long is the third side?

Solution

\(r^2 = 25 + 64 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 8 \cdot \cos 60^\circ = 89 - 80 \cdot \tfrac12 = 89 - 40 = 49\), so \(r = 7\). This is the demo's starting position.

Problem 4.2 medium

Sanity-check the law at the two extremes. What does it give for \(\ct{\gamma} = 0^\circ\) and for \(\ct{\gamma} = 180^\circ\), and why are those the right answers?

Solution

\(\ct{\gamma} = 0^\circ\): \(\cos = 1\), so \(r^2 = p^2 + q^2 - 2pq = (p - q)^2\) and \(r = |p - q|\). The hinge is shut, both arms lie on top of each other, and the gap between their ends is the difference in their lengths.

\(\ct{\gamma} = 180^\circ\): \(\cos = -1\), so \(r^2 = p^2 + q^2 + 2pq = (p + q)^2\) and \(r = p + q\). The hinge is fully open, the arms form one straight line, and the distance end to end is the sum.

Problem 4.3 medium

A triangle has sides 7, 8 and 13. What is the angle facing the side of length 13?

Hint

Solve the law of cosines for \(\cos\gamma\), then use the table.

Solution

Rearranged: \(\cos\gamma = \dfrac{p^2 + q^2 - r^2}{2pq} = \dfrac{49 + 64 - 169}{2 \cdot 7 \cdot 8} = \dfrac{-56}{112} = -\dfrac12\).

Which angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) has cosine \(-\tfrac12\)? Since \(\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12\), the second symmetry gives \(\cos 120^\circ = -\tfrac12\). So \(\gamma = 120^\circ\). The negative cosine was the tell that the angle is obtuse; that makes sense, since 13 is much longer than \(\sqrt{49 + 64} \approx 10.6\), the value a right angle would give.

05How much do two arrows agree?

Back to the main road. Forget the receipt for a while and start from a question about pictures.

You have two arrows, \(\va\) and \(\vb\), drawn from the same point. You want one number that says how much they go the same way. Before looking for a formula, write down what such a number should do:

  • If the arrows point the same way, it should be large and positive.
  • If they point opposite ways, it should be large and negative: they disagree as strongly as possible.
  • If they are at right angles, it should be zero: neither has anything to say about the other's direction.
  • Longer arrows should count for more. A strong push in the right direction beats a feeble one.

The stick and its shadow from section 3 already does most of this. There, the ground was the x-axis. Nothing stops us from tilting our head and using the line through \(\vb\) as the ground instead. Shine a light straight down onto that line and \(\va\) casts a shadow on it.

Definition

The shadow of \(\va\) on \(\vb\) (formally, the scalar projection) is the signed length

\[ \cp{\text{shadow}} = \norm{\va}\cos\th, \]

where \(\th\) is the angle between the two arrows, from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\). It is how far \(\va\) reaches in the direction of \(\vb\).

This is the same \(L\cos\th\) as before, with \(L = \norm{\va}\) and the angle measured from \(\vb\) instead of from the x-axis. Check it against the wish list. Same direction: \(\cos 0^\circ = 1\), the shadow is the whole arrow. Opposite: \(\cos 180^\circ = -1\), the shadow is the whole arrow, counted negative. Right angle: zero. A longer \(\va\): a proportionally longer shadow.

One item on the list is only half done. The shadow grows with the length of \(\va\), but it ignores the length of \(\vb\) completely: \(\vb\) only supplies a direction. That makes the measure lopsided. "How much does \(\va\) agree with \(\vb\)" and "how much does \(\vb\) agree with \(\va\)" would be different numbers, \(\norm{\va}\cos\th\) and \(\norm{\vb}\cos\th.\)

The repair is to multiply the shadow by the length of the arrow it falls on:

\[ \underbrace{\norm{\va}\cos\th}_{\cp{\text{shadow of a on b}}} \times \norm{\vb} \;=\; \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th \;=\; \underbrace{\norm{\vb}\cos\th}_{\cp{\text{shadow of b on a}}} \times \norm{\va}. \]

Now both lengths count, and the number is the same whichever arrow you treat as the ground. Is multiplying by \(\norm{\vb}\) natural, or just a trick to force symmetry? Physics says natural. Push a cart with a force \(\va\) while it rolls along a displacement \(\vb\). Only the part of the force along the direction of travel does anything useful, and that is the shadow. And pushing over twice the distance transfers twice the energy, so the distance multiplies in. Shadow times length is exactly the physicists' work.

Definition: the dot product, from the picture \[ \va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th \]

In words: the shadow of one arrow on the other, times the length of the other. If either arrow is the zero vector there is no angle to speak of, and the dot product is defined to be 0.

Note what kind of thing this is. Two vectors go in and a plain number comes out, not a vector. That is why it is also called the scalar product ("scalar" is the linear-algebra word for a plain number).

Here it is live. The readout computes the dot product twice: once from the picture as lengths and a cosine, and once by the receipt recipe on the components. Drag either arrowhead.

The agreement of those two lines is the claim from the introduction. We have not explained it yet. That is the next section.

Problem 5.1 easy

Compute \(\va\cdot\vb\) from the picture definition when (a) \(\norm{\va} = 6\), \(\norm{\vb} = 5\), \(\th = 60^\circ\); (b) \(\norm{\va} = 4\), \(\norm{\vb} = 3\), \(\th = 120^\circ\).

Solution

(a) \(6 \times 5 \times \cos 60^\circ = 30 \times \tfrac12 = 15\).

(b) \(4 \times 3 \times \cos 120^\circ = 12 \times (-\tfrac12) = -6\). Negative, because the arrows are more than a right angle apart. Forgetting that the cosine of an obtuse angle is negative gives \(+6\), the common wrong answer.

Problem 5.2 easy

\(\va\) has length 2 and \(\vb\) has length 3, and you may point them however you like. What are the largest and smallest possible values of \(\va\cdot\vb\), and how do you point them to get each? When is it zero?

Solution

\(\va\cdot\vb = 6\cos\th\), and \(\cos\th\) ranges over \([-1, 1]\). Largest: \(6\), when \(\th = 0^\circ\) (same direction). Smallest: \(-6\), when \(\th = 180^\circ\) (opposite). Zero when \(\th = 90^\circ\) (perpendicular).

The general lesson, which becomes the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in section 11: a dot product can never be bigger in size than the product of the lengths.

Problem 5.3 medium

What is the dot product of a vector with itself, \(\va\cdot\va\), according to the picture definition?

Solution

The angle between an arrow and itself is \(0^\circ\), and \(\cos 0^\circ = 1\). So \(\va\cdot\va = \norm{\va}\,\norm{\va} \cdot 1 = \norm{\va}^2\): the length squared. Turned around, \(\norm{\va} = \sqrt{\va\cdot\va}\). Length is a special case of the dot product, which will matter a lot later.

06Finding the formula

The picture definition is meaningful but awkward to compute. Given \(\va = (3, 1)\) and \(\vb = (2, 4)\), you would need both lengths (two square roots) and then the angle between them, and we do not even have a method for the angle yet. We want a way to get \(\va\cdot\vb\) directly from the components. Let us look for one, starting with a case so easy that the answer is visible.

Warm-up: when one arrow lies along an axis

Suppose \(\vb\) lies along the positive x-axis: \(\vb = (\cb{b_1}, 0)\) with \(\cb{b_1} > 0\). The ground is then the x-axis itself, and the shadow of \(\va = (\ca{a_1}, \ca{a_2})\) on the x-axis is simply \(\ca{a_1}\). That is what an x-component is: how far the arrow reaches along x. The length of \(\vb\) is \(\cb{b_1}\). So

\[ \va\cdot\vb = \cp{\text{shadow}} \times \norm{\vb} = \ca{a_1}\,\cb{b_1}. \]

If \(\cb{b_1}\) is negative, \(\vb\) points left. Its length is \(-\cb{b_1}\), and the shadow of \(\va\) measured along the leftward direction is \(-\ca{a_1}\). The product is \((-\ca{a_1})(-\cb{b_1}) = \ca{a_1}\cb{b_1}\) again. Likewise, if \(\vb = (0, \cb{b_2})\) lies along the y-axis, then \(\va\cdot\vb = \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2}\).

Two special cases of this deserve their own line. With \(\vv{e}_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(\vv{e}_2 = (0, 1)\):

\[ \va\cdot\vv{e}_1 = \ca{a_1}, \qquad \va\cdot\vv{e}_2 = \ca{a_2}. \]
Worth remembering

Dotting a vector with a basis vector reads off one component. A component is a shadow on an axis.

The key insight: break b into axis pieces

A general \(\vb\) does not lie along an axis, but it is a sum of two pieces that do:

\[ \vb = \cb{b_1}\,\vv{e}_1 + \cb{b_2}\,\vv{e}_2. \]

If the dot product could be taken piece by piece, so that the dot product with a sum is the sum of the dot products and a scaling factor can be pulled out front, we would be finished:

\[ \va\cdot\vb \;=\; \va\cdot(\cb{b_1}\vv{e}_1 + \cb{b_2}\vv{e}_2) \;\overset{?}{=}\; \cb{b_1}\,(\va\cdot\vv{e}_1) + \cb{b_2}\,(\va\cdot\vv{e}_2) \;=\; \cb{b_1}\ca{a_1} + \cb{b_2}\ca{a_2}. \]

And there is the receipt. So the entire mystery reduces to the step marked with a question mark. It needs two facts about the picture definition, and both are facts about shadows.

Fact 1: scaling an arrow scales the dot product

Claim: \(\va\cdot(k\,\vb) = k\,(\va\cdot\vb)\) for any number \(k\).

  • If \(k > 0\): the arrow \(k\vb\) points the same way as \(\vb\), so the angle \(\th\) is unchanged, and its length is \(k\norm{\vb}\). So \(\va\cdot(k\vb) = \norm{\va}\,(k\norm{\vb})\cos\th = k\,(\va\cdot\vb)\).
  • If \(k < 0\): the arrow \(k\vb\) points the opposite way. Its length is \(|k|\,\norm{\vb}\), and the angle to \(\va\) is now \(180^\circ - \th\). By the second symmetry of cosine, \(\cos(180^\circ - \th) = -\cos\th\). So \(\va\cdot(k\vb) = \norm{\va}\,|k|\,\norm{\vb}\,(-\cos\th)\), and since \(-|k| = k\) for negative \(k\), this is \(k\,(\va\cdot\vb)\) again.
  • If \(k = 0\): both sides are zero.

The picture definition is symmetric in its two arrows, so the same holds for scaling \(\va\).

Fact 2: shadows add

Claim: for any two arrows \(\vv{u}\) and \(\vv{v}\), the shadow of \(\vv{u} + \vv{v}\) on \(\vb\) is the shadow of \(\vv{u}\) plus the shadow of \(\vv{v}\).

Look first, then argue. Below, u and v are chained tip to tail, so the white arrow from the origin to the end of the chain is their sum. Under the line through b are the shadows: u's and v's laid end to end on one track, and the shadow of the sum on the track beneath.

Why must this be so? Rotate the whole sheet of paper until the line through \(\vb\) is horizontal. Rotating a sheet changes no lengths and no angles, so every shadow keeps its value. But now "the shadow on \(\vb\)" is "the reach along the horizontal axis", which is just the x-component in the rotated picture. And x-components add when vectors add; that is how vector addition is defined. So shadows add.

Multiply the statement through by \(\norm{\vb}\), and shadows become dot products:

\[ (\vv{u} + \vv{v})\cdot\vb = \vv{u}\cdot\vb + \vv{v}\cdot\vb. \]

By symmetry the same is true in the other slot: \(\va\cdot(\vv{u} + \vv{v}) = \va\cdot\vv{u} + \va\cdot\vv{v}\).

Putting it together

Now every step is justified. Write the reason next to each one:

\[ \begin{aligned} \va\cdot\vb &= \va\cdot(\cb{b_1}\vv{e}_1 + \cb{b_2}\vv{e}_2) && \text{split } \vb \text{ into axis pieces} \\ &= \va\cdot(\cb{b_1}\vv{e}_1) + \va\cdot(\cb{b_2}\vv{e}_2) && \text{Fact 2: shadows add} \\ &= \cb{b_1}\,(\va\cdot\vv{e}_1) + \cb{b_2}\,(\va\cdot\vv{e}_2) && \text{Fact 1: scaling pulls out} \\ &= \cb{b_1}\,\ca{a_1} + \cb{b_2}\,\ca{a_2} && \text{warm-up: dotting with } \vv{e}_i \text{ reads a component} \end{aligned} \]
The dot product, from the numbers \[ \va\cdot\vb = \ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2} \qquad\text{and in } n \text{ dimensions}\qquad \va\cdot\vb = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ca{a_i}\cb{b_i}. \]

In code, \(n\) is len(left) and the sum is sum(l * r for l, r in zip(left, right)).

Nothing in the argument was special to two dimensions. In three dimensions there is a third basis vector \(\vv{e}_3 = (0, 0, 1)\), shadows still add (rotate space until \(\vb\) lies along an axis), and the same four lines produce \(\ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2} + \ca{a_3}\cb{b_3}\).

So the receipt was never a coincidence. Multiplying matching components and adding is what you get when you compute "shadow times length" one axis at a time. Each product \(\ca{a_i}\cb{b_i}\) is the agreement of the two arrows along axis \(i\), and the total agreement is the sum over the axes.

Problem 6.1 easy

Compute \((2, -1, 3)\cdot(4, 5, 1)\).

Solution

\(2\cdot 4 + (-1)\cdot 5 + 3\cdot 1 = 8 - 5 + 3 = 6\). The answer is one number. Writing \((8, -5, 3)\) and stopping is the classic slip: that is the component-wise product, and the dot product is its sum.

Problem 6.2 easy

Without finding any angle, decide whether each pair meets at an acute, right or obtuse angle: (a) \((1, 2)\) and \((3, -1)\); (b) \((3, 4)\) and \((8, -6)\); (c) \((1, 2, 2)\) and \((-2, -1, 0)\).

Solution

Lengths are positive, so the sign of \(\norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th\) is the sign of \(\cos\th\), and that tells acute from obtuse.

(a) \(3 - 2 = 1 > 0\): acute (barely; it turns out to be about \(82^\circ\)).

(b) \(24 - 24 = 0\): a right angle.

(c) \(-2 - 2 + 0 = -4 < 0\): obtuse.

This is the cheapest and most used application of the dot product: three multiplications tell you which side of perpendicular you are on.

Problem 6.3 medium

For which value of \(k\) is \((2, k)\) perpendicular to \((3, -4)\)? Then: write down, by inspection, a nonzero vector perpendicular to \((a_1, a_2)\).

Solution

Perpendicular means the dot product is zero: \(2\cdot 3 + k\cdot(-4) = 0\), so \(6 = 4k\) and \(k = 1.5\).

For the second part, swap the components and negate one: \((-a_2, a_1)\). Check: \(a_1\cdot(-a_2) + a_2\cdot a_1 = 0\). This is the arrow rotated a quarter turn counter-clockwise, and it is a trick worth keeping. \((a_2, -a_1)\) works too (a quarter turn clockwise).

07A second proof

The argument of the last section explains why the formula has the shape it has. Here is a second, completely different proof. It is the one most textbooks give; it is shorter, more of a calculation, and it is what the law of cosines was built for. Having two independent routes to the same place is good evidence you have not fooled yourself.

Draw \(\va\) and \(\vb\) from the origin and join their tips. The joining side, running from the tip of \(\vb\) to the tip of \(\va\), is the vector \(\va - \vb\), because \(\vb + (\va - \vb) = \va\). The three arrows form a triangle, and the angle at the origin, between the sides \(\va\) and \(\vb\), is \(\th\).

Apply the law of cosines with \(p = \norm{\va}\), \(q = \norm{\vb}\), \(\gamma = \th\), and third side \(r = \norm{\va - \vb}\):

\[ \norm{\va - \vb}^2 = \norm{\va}^2 + \norm{\vb}^2 - 2\,\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th. \qquad (1) \]

Now compute the left side a second way, directly from components. The vector \(\va - \vb\) is \((\ca{a_1} - \cb{b_1},\ \ca{a_2} - \cb{b_2})\), and a squared length is the sum of squared components:

\[ \begin{aligned} \norm{\va - \vb}^2 &= (\ca{a_1} - \cb{b_1})^2 + (\ca{a_2} - \cb{b_2})^2 \\ &= \ca{a_1}^2 - 2\ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \cb{b_1}^2 + \ca{a_2}^2 - 2\ca{a_2}\cb{b_2} + \cb{b_2}^2 \\ &= \underbrace{(\ca{a_1}^2 + \ca{a_2}^2)}_{\norm{\va}^2} + \underbrace{(\cb{b_1}^2 + \cb{b_2}^2)}_{\norm{\vb}^2} - 2\,(\ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2}). \qquad (2) \end{aligned} \]

Lines (1) and (2) are two expressions for the same quantity, so they are equal. Both contain \(\norm{\va}^2 + \norm{\vb}^2\); subtract it from each:

\[ -2\,\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th = -2\,(\ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2}). \]

Divide both sides by \(-2\):

\[ \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th = \ca{a_1}\cb{b_1} + \ca{a_2}\cb{b_2}. \qquad\blacksquare \]

Seen this way, the dot product is precisely the correction term in the law of cosines, the thing that measures how far a triangle is from being right-angled. When \(\va\cdot\vb = 0\) the correction vanishes and (1) collapses to Pythagoras.

Check with the running example. \(\va - \vb = (1, -3)\), so \(\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = 1 + 9 = 10\). And \(\norm{\va}^2 + \norm{\vb}^2 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb = 10 + 20 - 2\cdot 10 = 10\). Both agree.

Problem 7.1 medium

The proof above was written for two components. Redo step (2) for vectors with three components and confirm that the conclusion becomes \(\norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3\). Which step of the proof, if any, needs a new idea in 3D?

Solution

\(\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = (a_1 - b_1)^2 + (a_2 - b_2)^2 + (a_3 - b_3)^2\). Each square expands as \(a_i^2 - 2a_ib_i + b_i^2\). Collecting: \((a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2) + (b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2) - 2(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3)\), which is \(\norm{\va}^2 + \norm{\vb}^2 - 2\sum a_ib_i\). Comparing with (1) and cancelling as before gives the three-term formula.

No new idea is needed. Two arrows from a common point always lie in one flat plane, even inside 3D space, so the triangle they form is an ordinary flat triangle and the law of cosines applies to it unchanged. Only the component bookkeeping grows.

Problem 7.2 medium

Two arrows have \(\norm{\va} = 5\), \(\norm{\vb} = 6\) and the distance between their tips is \(\norm{\va - \vb} = 7\). Find \(\va\cdot\vb\) without knowing any components.

Solution

Combine (1) with the definition \(\va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th\): \(\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = \norm{\va}^2 + \norm{\vb}^2 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb\). So \(49 = 25 + 36 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb\), giving \(2\,\va\cdot\vb = 12\) and \(\va\cdot\vb = 6\).

This is a useful trick in its own right: three lengths determine a dot product. It is how distances are turned into dot products (and back) in many algorithms.

08The rules of the game

With the component formula in hand, the dot product can be manipulated like ordinary multiplication in most ways, and unlike it in a few. Each rule below can be checked in one line from \(\sum a_ib_i\), and each also has a reading in the picture.

rulestatementwhy
Order does not matter\(\va\cdot\vb = \vb\cdot\va\)\(a_ib_i = b_ia_i\)
Sums split\(\va\cdot(\vv{u} + \vv{v}) = \va\cdot\vv{u} + \va\cdot\vv{v}\)shadows add
Scalars pull out\((k\va)\cdot\vb = k\,(\va\cdot\vb)\)\((ka_i)b_i = k(a_ib_i)\)
Self-product is length squared\(\va\cdot\va = \norm{\va}^2\)\(\sum a_i^2\)
Never negative on itself\(\va\cdot\va \ge 0\), zero only for \(\va = \vv{0}\)sum of squares

"Sums split" and "scalars pull out" together are called linearity, and since they hold in each slot separately the dot product is called bilinear. In practice it means you may expand brackets exactly as in school algebra. The most useful expansion of all:

\[ \begin{aligned} \norm{\va + \vb}^2 &= (\va + \vb)\cdot(\va + \vb) && \text{length squared is a self-product} \\ &= \va\cdot\va + \va\cdot\vb + \vb\cdot\va + \vb\cdot\vb && \text{sums split, twice} \\ &= \norm{\va}^2 + 2\,\va\cdot\vb + \norm{\vb}^2 && \text{order does not matter} \end{aligned} \]

It looks like \((x + y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2\) and it is proved the same way. With a minus sign, \(\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = \norm{\va}^2 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb + \norm{\vb}^2\), which is the law of cosines again, now derived in one line of algebra.

Where the analogy with multiplication breaks

Three things you cannot do

No chains. \(\va\cdot\vb\cdot\vv{c}\) is meaningless. \(\va\cdot\vb\) is a number, and a number cannot be dotted with the vector \(\vv{c}\). You can scale \(\vv{c}\) by that number, \((\va\cdot\vb)\,\vv{c}\), but that is a different operation, and it is not equal to \(\va\,(\vb\cdot\vv{c})\): one points along \(\vv{c}\), the other along \(\va\).

No cancelling. From \(\va\cdot\vb = \va\cdot\vv{c}\) you may not conclude \(\vb = \vv{c}\). It only says \(\va\cdot(\vb - \vv{c}) = 0\), that is, \(\vb - \vv{c}\) is perpendicular to \(\va\). Many different arrows cast the same shadow.

No division. There is no "\(\va / \vb\)". Knowing \(\va\cdot\vb\) and \(\vb\) does not determine \(\va\), for the same reason.

Problem 8.1 easy

Given \(\norm{\va} = 3\), \(\norm{\vb} = 4\) and \(\va\cdot\vb = -2\), find \(\norm{\va + \vb}\).

Solution

\(\norm{\va + \vb}^2 = 9 + 2(-2) + 16 = 21\), so \(\norm{\va + \vb} = \sqrt{21} \approx 4.583\). The tempting wrong answers are \(7\) (adding lengths, true only for parallel arrows) and \(5\) (Pythagoras, true only for perpendicular ones). The dot product is what interpolates between those.

Problem 8.2 medium

Find three vectors in the plane with \(\va\cdot\vb = \va\cdot\vv{c}\) but \(\vb \ne \vv{c}\) and \(\va \neq \vv{0}\).

Solution

Pick \(\va = (1, 0)\). Dotting with it reads the first component, so any two vectors with the same first component work: \(\vb = (2, 5)\), \(\vv{c} = (2, -7)\). Both dot products are 2. Their difference \((0, 12)\) is perpendicular to \(\va\), as the warning box said it must be.

Problem 8.3 medium, a proof

A parallelogram has sides \(\va\) and \(\vb\); its diagonals are \(\va + \vb\) and \(\va - \vb\). Prove that the diagonals are perpendicular exactly when the two sides have equal length (that is, when the parallelogram is a rhombus).

Hint

Expand \((\va + \vb)\cdot(\va - \vb)\).

Solution

\((\va + \vb)\cdot(\va - \vb) = \va\cdot\va - \va\cdot\vb + \vb\cdot\va - \vb\cdot\vb\). The middle terms cancel because order does not matter, leaving \(\norm{\va}^2 - \norm{\vb}^2\).

The diagonals are perpendicular exactly when this dot product is zero, that is when \(\norm{\va}^2 = \norm{\vb}^2\), and since lengths are non-negative, when \(\norm{\va} = \norm{\vb}\). Note how little geometry was needed: one expansion.

09Lengths and angles, by hand

Run the main equation backwards. If both formulas give the same number, then the cheap one can be used to find the angle, which a moment ago we had no way to compute. Divide \(\va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th\) by the two lengths:

Angle between two vectors \[ \cos\th = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}}, \qquad \th = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}}\right). \]

Both vectors must be nonzero. Everything on the right is made of dot products, since \(\norm{\va} = \sqrt{\va\cdot\va}\).

A full computation

Take \(\va = (\ca{3}, \ca{1})\) and \(\vb = (\cb{2}, \cb{4})\), the starting position of the demo, and do every step.

  1. Dot product. \(\va\cdot\vb = \ca{3}\cdot\cb{2} + \ca{1}\cdot\cb{4} = 6 + 4 = 10\).
  2. Lengths. \(\norm{\va} = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162\). \(\norm{\vb} = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.472\).
  3. Cosine. \(\cos\th = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{10}\,\sqrt{20}} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{200}} = \dfrac{10}{10\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.7071\).
  4. Angle. The table in section 3 says \(\cos 45^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), so \(\th = 45^\circ\).
  5. Shadows, for free. The shadow of \(\va\) on \(\vb\) is \(\dfrac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\vb}} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{20}} \approx 2.236\). The shadow of \(\vb\) on \(\va\) is \(\dfrac{10}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 3.162\).

The same thing in code. The one line that is not a transcription of the formula is the clamp; it is there because of the floating-point issue from Problem 3.6.

import math


def dot(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(left_entry * right_entry for left_entry, right_entry in zip(left, right, strict=True))


def length(vector: list[float]) -> float:
    return math.sqrt(dot(vector, vector))


def angle_between(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    """Angle in degrees, from 0 to 180. Undefined when either vector is zero."""
    left_length, right_length = length(left), length(right)
    if left_length == 0 or right_length == 0:
        raise ValueError("the zero vector has no direction, so no angle")
    cosine = dot(left, right) / (left_length * right_length)
    cosine = max(-1.0, min(1.0, cosine))  # rounding can push it to 1.0000000000000002
    return math.degrees(math.acos(cosine))

yellow_arrow: list[float] = [2, 4]

print(f"lengths       {length(blue_arrow):.4f} and {length(yellow_arrow):.4f}")
print(f"angle         {angle_between(blue_arrow, yellow_arrow):.4f} degrees")
print(f"perpendicular {angle_between([3, 4], [8, -6]):.4f} degrees")

But the formula does not care: it reports that this vector makes a \(45^\circ\) angle with \((1, 1, 0, 0)\). Section 11 looks at what gives us the right to call that number an angle.

Problem 9.1 easy

Find the angle between \((1, 0)\) and \((1, \sqrt3)\).

Solution

Dot product: \(1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot\sqrt3 = 1\). Lengths: \(1\) and \(\sqrt{1 + 3} = 2\). So \(\cos\th = 1/2\) and \(\th = 60^\circ\). (This is the equilateral-triangle picture from section 3, scaled by 2.)

Problem 9.2 medium

In a cube, what is the angle between the long diagonal (corner to opposite corner, through the middle) and an edge? Use the cube with corners at coordinates 0 and 1.

Solution

From the corner at the origin, the long diagonal is \((1, 1, 1)\) and an edge is \((1, 0, 0)\). Dot product: \(1\). Lengths: \(\sqrt3\) and \(1\). So \(\cos\th = 1/\sqrt3 \approx 0.5774\) and \(\th = \arccos(0.5774) \approx 54.74^\circ\).

A tempting guess is \(45^\circ\), by analogy with the diagonal of a square. But the cube's diagonal leans away from each edge more than a square's diagonal does, because it has to share itself among three axes and not two.

Problem 9.3 medium, predict the output

What does angle_between([2, 0], [-3, 0]) return, and what about angle_between([1, 2], [2, 4])? What would happen in the second call if the clamp line were deleted?

Solution

First call: dot product \(-6\), lengths 2 and 3, cosine \(-1\), angle \(180.0\). The arrows point in opposite directions.

Second call: \((2, 4) = 2\cdot(1, 2)\), so the arrows are parallel and the answer should be \(0.0\). The cosine is \(10 / (\sqrt5 \cdot \sqrt{20})\), which in exact arithmetic is 1. In floating point, \(\sqrt5 \cdot \sqrt{20}\) comes out as 10.000000000000002, so the quotient here is 0.9999999999999998 and acos returns about \(1.2\times10^{-6}\) degrees: harmless. But for other parallel pairs the rounding lands on the other side of 1: [1, 5] against itself gives a cosine of 1.0000000000000002. Then, without the clamp, math.acos raises ValueError. A crash that depends on the last bit of a float is exactly the kind of bug that survives testing, hence the clamp.

10Projection

So far the shadow has been a length. Often you want the shadow as an arrow: the vector that lies along \(\vb\) and is as close to \(\va\) as anything along \(\vb\) can be. It is called the projection of \(\va\) onto \(\vb\), and building it takes two steps.

Step 1: how long. The signed length of the shadow, from \(\va\cdot\vb = \cp{\text{shadow}} \times \norm{\vb}\):

\[ \cp{\text{shadow}} = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\vb}}. \]

Step 2: which way. Along \(\vb\). The unit vector in that direction is \(\vb / \norm{\vb}\). An arrow of signed length \(\cp{\text{shadow}}\) in that direction is the shadow times the unit vector:

\[ \cp{\operatorname{proj}_{\vb}\va} = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\vb}} \cdot \frac{\vb}{\norm{\vb}} = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\vb}^2}\,\vb = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\vb\cdot\vb}\,\vb. \]
Projection \[ \cp{\operatorname{proj}_{\vb}\va} = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\vb\cdot\vb}\,\vb \]

A ratio of two dot products (a plain number) times the vector \(\vb\). No square roots and no cosines are needed. If \(\vb\) is already a unit vector, the bottom is 1 and it simplifies to \((\va\cdot\vb)\,\vb\).

Whatever is left of \(\va\) after removing its projection must be the part of \(\va\) that has nothing to do with \(\vb\):

\[ \cs{\va_\perp} = \va - \cp{\operatorname{proj}_{\vb}\va}. \]

This leftover is perpendicular to \(\vb\), and we can prove it by computing a dot product, which should be zero:

\[ \cs{\va_\perp}\cdot\vb = \left(\va - \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\vb\cdot\vb}\,\vb\right)\cdot\vb = \va\cdot\vb - \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\vb\cdot\vb}\,(\vb\cdot\vb) = \va\cdot\vb - \va\cdot\vb = 0. \]

So every vector splits cleanly into a piece along \(\vb\) and a piece across \(\vb\), and the two pieces add back up to \(\va\).

You are given two vectors \(\va\) and \(\vb\) in the plane that are not parallel. Describe how to build two vectors that are perpendicular to each other, both of length 1, and such that the first points along \(\vb\). (This two-step recipe, repeated for more vectors, is the Gram–Schmidt process.)

Solution

11Cauchy–Schwarz: why angles exist in any dimension

There is a loose thread. In two and three dimensions we defined the dot product by \(\norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th\) with a real angle you can measure, and then proved it equals \(\sum a_ib_i\). In 4 or 768 dimensions there is no protractor. There, the logic runs the other way: the sum is the definition, and the angle is defined by

\[ \th = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}}\right). \]

That definition has a hole in it. Arccosine only accepts inputs between \(-1\) and \(1\). In the plane the fraction is \(\cos\th\) and is obviously in range. But in 768 dimensions, with no picture, how do we know that the fraction never comes out as, say, 1.3? If it could, "the angle between two embeddings" would be nonsense.

It cannot, and the guarantee is one of the most used inequalities in mathematics.

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality \[ |\va\cdot\vb| \;\le\; \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb} \]

for vectors of any dimension, with equality exactly when one vector is a multiple of the other.

Proof, using only algebra

The proof must not mention angles, since their existence is what we are trying to establish. It may use only the rules from section 8, which follow from the sum formula. The idea is borrowed from the last section: the leftover after projecting has a length, and a squared length cannot be negative.

If \(\vb = \vv{0}\) both sides are 0 and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise let \(t = \dfrac{\va\cdot\vb}{\vb\cdot\vb}\) and \(\cs{\vv{r}} = \va - t\,\vb\) (the leftover). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 \;\le\; \cs{\vv{r}}\cdot\cs{\vv{r}} &= (\va - t\vb)\cdot(\va - t\vb) && \text{a self-product is never negative} \\ &= \va\cdot\va - 2t\,(\va\cdot\vb) + t^2\,(\vb\cdot\vb) && \text{expand the brackets} \\ &= \va\cdot\va - 2\,\frac{(\va\cdot\vb)^2}{\vb\cdot\vb} + \frac{(\va\cdot\vb)^2}{(\vb\cdot\vb)^2}\,(\vb\cdot\vb) && \text{substitute } t \\ &= \va\cdot\va - \frac{(\va\cdot\vb)^2}{\vb\cdot\vb}. && -2x + x = -x \end{aligned} \]

So \(0 \le \va\cdot\va - \dfrac{(\va\cdot\vb)^2}{\vb\cdot\vb}\). Move the fraction across and multiply by the positive number \(\vb\cdot\vb\):

\[ (\va\cdot\vb)^2 \le (\va\cdot\va)(\vb\cdot\vb) = \norm{\va}^2\,\norm{\vb}^2. \]

Take square roots of both sides (both are non-negative): \(|\va\cdot\vb| \le \norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\). Equality holds exactly when \(\cs{\vv{r}}\cdot\cs{\vv{r}} = 0\), which means the leftover is the zero vector, which means \(\va = t\vb\). \(\blacksquare\)

In pictures, the proof says: a shadow is never longer than the thing casting it. That is obvious on paper. The value of the algebraic proof is that it makes the statement true in spaces where there is no paper.

A corollary: the triangle inequality

"The direct route is never longer than a detour": \(\norm{\va + \vb} \le \norm{\va} + \norm{\vb}\). From the expansion in section 8 and Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \norm{\va + \vb}^2 = \norm{\va}^2 + 2\,\va\cdot\vb + \norm{\vb}^2 \;\le\; \norm{\va}^2 + 2\,\norm{\va}\norm{\vb} + \norm{\vb}^2 = (\norm{\va} + \norm{\vb})^2, \]

and taking square roots gives the claim. So in any number of dimensions, lengths behave like lengths and angles behave like angles, and it all rests on the single fact that a sum of squares is not negative.

Problem 11.1 easy

Verify Cauchy–Schwarz for \(\va = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\vb = (4, 5, 6)\), and find the angle between them.

Solution

\(\va\cdot\vb = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32\). \(\norm{\va} = \sqrt{14}\), \(\norm{\vb} = \sqrt{77}\), product \(\sqrt{1078} \approx 32.83\). Indeed \(32 \le 32.83\). The ratio is \(0.9746\), and \(\arccos(0.9746) \approx 12.93^\circ\). The two vectors are nearly parallel, which is why the inequality is nearly an equality.

Problem 11.2 hard

Prove that for any three real numbers, \((x + y + z)^2 \le 3\,(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)\). When does equality hold?

Hint

Write \(x + y + z\) as a dot product of \((x, y, z)\) with a cleverly chosen fixed vector.

Solution

\(x + y + z = (x, y, z)\cdot(1, 1, 1)\). By Cauchy–Schwarz in squared form, \(\big((x, y, z)\cdot(1, 1, 1)\big)^2 \le \norm{(x, y, z)}^2\,\norm{(1, 1, 1)}^2 = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)\cdot 3\).

Equality holds when \((x, y, z)\) is a multiple of \((1, 1, 1)\), that is when \(x = y = z.\) This "dot with the all-ones vector" trick turns many inequalities about sums and averages into one-liners; the same argument with \(n\) numbers shows that the square of a mean is at most the mean of the squares.

Is it in front of me? The vision cone

A guard in a stealth game stands at some point and looks in a direction f. A thief is somewhere else; the arrow from the guard to the thief is t. The guard has a field of view of, say, \(90^\circ\): \(45^\circ\) to each side of where they are looking. Does the guard see the thief?

The direct approach computes the angle between \(\vv{f}\) and \(\vv{t}\) and compares it with \(45^\circ\). That costs an arccosine per guard per thief per frame. But we do not need the angle, only a comparison, and cosine is a decreasing function between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) (look back at the curve in section 3): a smaller angle means a larger cosine. So

\[ \text{angle} \le 45^\circ \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \cos(\text{angle}) \ge \cos 45^\circ \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \hat{\vv{f}}\cdot\hat{\vv{t}} \ge 0.7071. \]

The threshold \(\cos 45^\circ\) is computed once. Each test is then a dot product of unit vectors and a comparison. Note that the inequality flips direction: inside the cone means above the threshold.

The crudest version of this test needs no threshold at all: the sign of \(\vv{f}\cdot\vv{t}\) says whether the target is in front (positive) or behind (negative). Games use that constantly, for example to skip drawing triangles that face away from the camera.

import math
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Point:
    x: float
    y: float


def can_see(guard: Point, facing: Point, target: Point, field_of_view_degrees: float) -> bool:
    """True when `target` is inside the guard's vision cone. No arccos needed."""
    to_target = Point(target.x - guard.x, target.y - guard.y)
    facing_length = math.hypot(facing.x, facing.y)
    target_distance = math.hypot(to_target.x, to_target.y)
    if target_distance == 0:
        return True
    cosine_of_angle = (facing.x * to_target.x + facing.y * to_target.y) / (facing_length * target_distance)
    cosine_of_half_cone = math.cos(math.radians(field_of_view_degrees / 2))
    return cosine_of_angle >= cosine_of_half_cone

How bright is a surface? Lambert's cosine law

Hold a sheet of paper under a lamp. Facing the lamp squarely, it is bright. Tilt it and it dims; edge-on, it gets no light. The reason is geometric. A beam of light of a given width carries a fixed amount of energy. Hitting the paper square on, the beam lands on a patch as wide as itself. Hitting at a slant, the same beam is smeared over a longer patch, so each bit of paper gets less.

How much less? Describe the tilt by the surface's normal n, the unit vector sticking straight out of it, and let l be the unit vector pointing from the surface toward the light. If \(\th\) is the angle between them, a beam of width \(w\) lands on a patch of length \(w / \cos\th\), so the light per unit of surface is multiplied by \(\cos\th\). That is the same shadow relationship as in section 3, run in reverse. And for unit vectors \(\cos\th\) is simply their dot product:

\[ \text{brightness} = \max(0,\ \ca{\vv{n}}\cdot\cb{\vv{l}}). \]

The \(\max\) with zero handles surfaces facing away from the light, where the dot product is negative: they receive nothing, not "negative light". This is called Lambertian or diffuse shading, and it is the first line of nearly every shader ever written.

Nothing in that picture is a 3D model. It is a flat disc of pixels. For the pixel at \((x, y)\) inside the disc, the normal of a unit sphere at that spot is \((x, y, \sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2})\), and one dot product with the light direction turns it into a shade. The roundness you see is entirely the cosine.

How far from a line?

A line in the plane can be described by a point \(\vv{p}_0\) on it and a normal n perpendicular to it. To find the distance from some other point \(\vv{p}\) to the line, take the arrow from \(\vv{p}_0\) to \(\vv{p}\) and ask how much of it sticks out across the line, that is, along the normal. That is a shadow:

\[ \text{signed distance} = (\vv{p} - \vv{p}_0)\cdot\hat{\vv{n}}. \]

The sign says which side of the line \(\vv{p}\) is on. The same formula, unchanged, gives the distance from a point to a plane in 3D, or to a hyperplane in any dimension.

This also explains the familiar equation of a line. The line \(3x + 4y = 12\) is the set of points \(\vv{p} = (x, y)\) with \((3, 4)\cdot\vv{p} = 12\): all points whose shadow on the direction \((3, 4)\) has the same fixed length. The coefficients of a line's equation are its normal vector.

Work

The physical quantity that motivated the definition in section 5: a constant force \(\vv{F}\) acting on an object that moves through a displacement \(\vv{d}\) does work \(W = \vv{F}\cdot\vv{d}\). Pulling a sled with a rope angled upward, only the forward part of your pull moves the sled. Carrying a suitcase horizontally at constant speed, the force you exert is vertical, the motion is horizontal, and in the physicist's sense you do no work on it at all, whatever your arms report.

Problem 12.1 easy

A guard at the origin faces \((1, 0)\) with a \(120^\circ\) field of view. Is a thief at \((1, 2)\) seen?

Solution

Half the cone is \(60^\circ\), and \(\cos 60^\circ = 0.5\). The cosine of the angle to the thief is \(\dfrac{(1, 0)\cdot(1, 2)}{1\cdot\sqrt5} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt5} \approx 0.447\). Since \(0.447 < 0.5\), the thief is outside the cone: not seen. (The actual angle is about \(63.4^\circ\), just past the \(60^\circ\) edge.) The classic bug is writing the comparison the wrong way round, because a bigger cosine means a smaller angle.

Problem 12.2 easy

A floor has normal \((0, 0, 1)\). A lamp is in the direction \((3, 0, 4)\) from a point on the floor. What fraction of full brightness does that point get? What if the lamp were in direction \((3, 0, -4)\)?

Solution

Normalise the light direction: its length is 5, so \(\vv{l} = (0.6, 0, 0.8)\). Then \(\vv{n}\cdot\vv{l} = 0.8\): eighty percent. Forgetting to normalise gives 4, a "brightness" of 400%.

For \((3, 0, -4)\) the dot product is \(-0.8\); the lamp is under the floor, and \(\max(0, -0.8) = 0\).

Problem 12.3 medium

Find the distance from the point \((5, 5)\) to the line \(x + y = 4\).

Solution

The normal is the coefficient vector \((1, 1)\), with length \(\sqrt2\). A point on the line: \((4, 0)\). Offset: \((5, 5) - (4, 0) = (1, 5)\). Signed distance: \(\dfrac{(1, 5)\cdot(1, 1)}{\sqrt2} = \dfrac{6}{\sqrt2} \approx 4.243\).

Shortcut: \((\vv{p} - \vv{p}_0)\cdot\vv{n} = \vv{p}\cdot\vv{n} - \vv{p}_0\cdot\vv{n} = (5 + 5) - 4\), because every point on the line has \(\vv{p}_0\cdot\vv{n} = 4\). So the distance is \(\dfrac{x + y - 4}{\sqrt2}\) evaluated at the point: plug the point into the line's equation and divide by the normal's length.

Problem 12.4 easy

A force \(\vv{F} = (3, 4)\) newtons acts on a cart that rolls along \(\vv{d} = (5, 0)\) metres. How much work is done? What force along the direction of motion would do the same work?

Solution

\(W = 3\cdot 5 + 4\cdot 0 = 15\) joules. The force has magnitude 5 N, but only its 3 N shadow on the direction of travel counts; a 3 N push straight along the track does the same work.

13Uses: machine learning

Modern machine learning runs on dot products to a degree that is hard to overstate. A large language model answering a question performs many billions of multiply-and-add steps, nearly all of them organised as dot products. Three places where it shows most clearly:

Cosine similarity: are these two things alike?

Turn a document into a vector by counting words: one component per word in the vocabulary, holding how often it appears. Two documents about the same topic use many of the same words, so their vectors have large entries in the same positions, and their dot product is large. The receipt logic again: each term \(\ca{a_i}\cb{b_i}\) is large only when word \(i\) is frequent in both.

But the raw dot product has a flaw as a similarity score. A document pasted three times has a vector three times longer and so a dot product three times larger, though it is not three times more relevant. The fix is to divide out both lengths, and that leaves the cosine of the angle:

\[ \text{cosine similarity}(\va, \vb) = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}} = \cos\th. \]

It runs from \(-1\) (opposite) through \(0\) (unrelated) to \(1\) (same direction), and ignores length entirely. For word counts, which are never negative, it stays between 0 and 1.

import math

    return sum(left_entry * right_entry for left_entry, right_entry in zip(left, right, strict=True))


def cosine_similarity(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    return dot(left, right) / (math.sqrt(dot(left, left)) * math.sqrt(dot(right, right)))

A fact about high dimensions

In 2D, two random arrows are often fairly well aligned by chance. In high dimensions that stops being true. For two random directions in \(n\) dimensions, the cosine is typically of size about \(1/\sqrt{n}\): around \(0.03\) for \(n = 1000\). Nearly all pairs of directions are nearly perpendicular. That is what makes a cosine of \(0.4\) between two embeddings meaningful: it is very unlikely to be an accident. Homework 3 asks you to measure this.

A neuron is a dot product

The basic unit of a neural network takes a list of inputs x, holds a list of learned weights w of the same length and one extra number called the bias, and outputs

\[ \text{output} = f(\cb{\vv{w}}\cdot\ca{\vv{x}} + \text{bias}), \]

where \(f\) is some simple fixed function, often \(\max(0, \cdot)\), known as ReLU. Everything interesting is inside the bracket, and it is a dot product. The weights are a direction the neuron is listening for. The dot product measures how far the input reaches in that direction, and the bias sets how far it must reach before the neuron responds.

The geometry is worth seeing once. The set of inputs where \(\cb{\vv{w}}\cdot\ca{\vv{x}} + \text{bias} = 0\) is a line (in 2D) whose normal is \(\cb{\vv{w}}\): this is the line equation from the last section. On one side the neuron fires; on the other it is silent. And \((\cb{\vv{w}}\cdot\ca{\vv{x}} + \text{bias}) / \norm{\cb{\vv{w}}}\) is the signed distance of the input from that line.

def dot(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(left_entry * right_entry for left_entry, right_entry in zip(left, right, strict=True))


def neuron(weights: list[float], bias: float, inputs: list[float]) -> float:
    """One artificial neuron with a ReLU: a dot product, a shift, and a clamp at zero."""
    return max(0.0, dot(weights, inputs) + bias)

A layer of a network is many neurons looking at the same input, each with its own weight vector, which is to say many dot products at once. That is a matrix times a vector, and the next section makes the connection exact.

Attention scores

The transformer architecture behind current language models has a step called attention in which each word decides how much to draw on every other word. The mechanism is a dot product used as a relevance score. Each word emits a query vector ("what I am looking for") and a key vector ("what I offer"). The relevance of word \(j\) to word \(i\) is

\[ \text{score}_{ij} = \frac{\vv{q}_i\cdot\vv{k}_j}{\sqrt{d}}, \]

where \(d\) is the number of components in each vector. The scores are then passed through softmax, a function that turns any list of numbers into positive weights that sum to 1 while keeping their order. The division by \(\sqrt d\) is there because a dot product is a sum of \(d\) terms and so tends to grow with \(d\); without it the scores would be large and softmax would put almost all the weight on one word.

import math


def dot(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(left_entry * right_entry for left_entry, right_entry in zip(left, right, strict=True))


def softmax(scores: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    largest = max(scores)
    exponentials = [math.exp(score - largest) for score in scores]
    total = sum(exponentials)
    return [value / total for value in exponentials]


# Made-up 3-number vectors: [is about animals, is about motion, is about money]
keys: dict[str, list[float]] = {
    "cat": [2.0, 0.3, 0.0],
    "ran": [0.2, 2.0, 0.0],
    "bank": [0.0, 0.1, 2.0],
}
query_for_it: list[float] = [1.8, 0.4, 0.1]  # the word "it" asking: which animal am I?
key_dimension = len(query_for_it)

scores = [dot(query_for_it, key) / math.sqrt(key_dimension) for key in keys.values()]
weights = softmax(scores)
for word, score, weight in zip(keys, scores, weights):
    print(f"it -> {word:<5} score {score:5.2f}   attention weight {weight:.3f}")
it -> cat   score  2.15   attention weight 0.734
it -> ran   score  0.67   attention weight 0.167
it -> bank  score  0.14   attention weight 0.098

With these hand-made vectors, the word "it" puts about 73% of its attention on "cat", because its query points in nearly the same direction as that key. In a real model nobody writes the vectors by hand; training adjusts them until the dot products pick out useful relationships.

Problem 13.2 medium

A neuron has weights \(\vv{w} = (2, -1)\) and bias \(-3\). (a) Does it fire (output above zero) on the input \((4, 1)\)? (b) How far is its decision boundary from the origin? (c) Give an input on the boundary.

Solution

(a) \(2\cdot 4 + (-1)\cdot 1 - 3 = 4 > 0\). It fires.

(b) The boundary is \(\vv{w}\cdot\vv{x} = 3\). By the distance formula the origin's signed distance is \((\vv{w}\cdot\vv{0} - 3)/\norm{\vv{w}} = -3/\sqrt5 \approx -1.342\). So the boundary is \(1.342\) from the origin, and the origin is on the silent side.

(c) Any \(\vv{x}\) with \(2x_1 - x_2 = 3\), for example \((1.5, 0)\) or \((2, 1)\).

Problem 13.3 medium

A search system normalises every stored embedding to length 1 when it is saved, and normalises the query too. Explain why ranking by plain dot product then gives the same order as ranking by cosine similarity, and also the same order as ranking by smallest straight-line distance.

Solution

For unit vectors the denominator of cosine similarity is \(1 \cdot 1\), so cosine similarity is the dot product.

For distance, use the expansion from section 8: \(\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = \norm{\va}^2 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb + \norm{\vb}^2 = 2 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb\) when both lengths are 1. Distance squared is a decreasing function of the dot product, so the largest dot product is the smallest distance. All three rankings agree, which is why systems normalise once up front and then use the cheapest of the three.

14Links into linear algebra

The dot product is the atom out of which most of linear algebra's operations are built. Three connections, each of which makes a later topic easier.

Matrix multiplication is a grid of dot products

Return to the receipt, and suppose there are two shoppers and two shops. Put the quantities in a table with one row per shopper, and the prices in a table with one column per shop. The bill for shopper \(i\) at shop \(j\) is a receipt total: row \(i\) of the first table dotted with column \(j\) of the second. Computing all four bills at once is matrix multiplication.

Definition

The entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) of the product \(AB\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\).

This is why the shapes must match in the way they do: a row of \(A\) and a column of \(B\) must have the same number of entries (here, 3 items) for the dot product to exist.

type Matrix = list[list[float]]


def dot(left: list[float], right: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(left_entry * right_entry for left_entry, right_entry in zip(left, right, strict=True))


def column(matrix: Matrix, column_index: int) -> list[float]:
    return [row[column_index] for row in matrix]


def matrix_multiply(left: Matrix, right: Matrix) -> Matrix:
    """Entry (row_index, column_index) is: row of `left` dotted with column of `right`."""
    column_count = len(right[0])
    return [[dot(left_row, column(right, column_index)) for column_index in range(column_count)] for left_row in left]


# Rows: two shoppers. Columns: apples, bread, milk.
quantities: Matrix = [[3, 2, 4], [1, 0, 6]]
# Rows: apples, bread, milk. Columns: price at shop A, price at shop B.
prices: Matrix = [[0.5, 0.6], [2.25, 2.0], [1.2, 1.0]]

    print([round(total, 2) for total in shopper_totals])
[10.8, 9.8]
[7.7, 6.6]

A matrix times a single vector is the special case with one column: each output entry is one row of the matrix dotted with the vector. So a neural-network layer, "every neuron's weights dotted with the input", is one matrix-vector product, with the neurons' weight vectors stacked as rows. In matrix notation the dot product itself is written \(\va^{\mathsf T}\vb\): the first vector laid on its side as a single row, times the second as a single column. In NumPy it is a @ b or np.dot(a, b).

Every linear function to a number is a dot product

Consider functions \(f\) that take a vector and return a number, and that are linear: \(f(\vv{u} + \vv{v}) = f(\vv{u}) + f(\vv{v})\) and \(f(k\vv{v}) = k f(\vv{v})\). The receipt total, as a function of the quantities, is one; "the x-component" is another. For a fixed \(\vv{w}\), the function \(\vv{x} \mapsto \vv{w}\cdot\vv{x}\) is always linear (those are the rules in section 8). The surprise is the converse: there are no others.

Here is the whole proof, in three dimensions. Write the input as a mix of basis vectors and use linearity:

\[ f(\vv{x}) = f(x_1\vv{e}_1 + x_2\vv{e}_2 + x_3\vv{e}_3) = x_1 f(\vv{e}_1) + x_2 f(\vv{e}_2) + x_3 f(\vv{e}_3). \]

The three numbers \(f(\vv{e}_1), f(\vv{e}_2), f(\vv{e}_3)\) do not depend on \(\vv{x}\). Collect them into a vector \(\vv{w}\), and the right side is \(\vv{w}\cdot\vv{x}\). So a linear function to a number is completely described by a single vector, found by feeding it the basis vectors, and applying the function is dotting with that vector. This correspondence between linear functions and vectors is called duality.

It is the same move as in section 6, where we broke \(\vb\) into axis pieces. And it explains why the dot product is unavoidable: anywhere a quantity depends linearly on a list of numbers, such as a price total, a weighted score, or a neuron's input, a dot product is being computed whether or not anyone calls it that.

Coordinates in a rotated frame

A set of vectors is orthonormal if each has length 1 and every pair is perpendicular. In dot-product language: each one dotted with itself gives 1, and dotted with any other gives 0. The standard basis \(\vv{e}_1, \vv{e}_2\) is the obvious example, but any rotated copy of it works equally well.

Orthonormal frames have a wonderful property: to find a vector's coordinates in the frame, just dot it with each frame vector. This is "dotting with a basis vector reads off a component" from section 6, and it holds in any orthonormal frame because the argument there used nothing but lengths and right angles.

A spaceship sits at the origin, tilted \(30^\circ\) from the map's x-axis. Its own "forward" and "left" directions form an orthonormal frame. An asteroid is at map position \((4, 2)\). Where is it from the pilot's point of view?

Two dot products convert map coordinates into ship coordinates; no equations to solve. The last line shows something further: the length of the vector comes out the same in either frame. More is true. If you convert two vectors into the ship's frame, their dot product is the same as it was in map coordinates. Rotating the frame changes every individual component and leaves every dot product alone. This is the algebraic form of the fact we used in section 6, that turning the sheet of paper changes no lengths or angles, and it is why the dot product is a statement about the arrows themselves and not about the coordinates used to write them down.

Compute \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5 & 6\\ 7 & 8\end{pmatrix}\) as four dot products.

Solution

Rows of the first: \((1, 2)\), \((3, 4)\). Columns of the second: \((5, 7)\), \((6, 8)\).

Problem 14.2 medium

(a) The function \(f(x, y, z) = 2x + 5y - z\) is linear. Which vector \(\vv{w}\) represents it, and how would you find \(\vv{w}\) if \(f\) were a black box you could only call? (b) Is \(g(x, y) = x + y + 1\) a dot product with some fixed vector?

Solution

(a) \(\vv{w} = (2, 5, -1)\). With a black box, call it on the basis vectors: \(f(1, 0, 0) = 2\), \(f(0, 1, 0) = 5\), \(f(0, 0, 1) = -1\). Three calls recover the whole function.

15Generalisations

Section 11 proved Cauchy–Schwarz, and with it the existence of angles, using nothing but three algebraic rules. That has a striking consequence. Anything obeying those rules comes with lengths, angles, perpendicularity and projection, whether or not it looks like an arrow.

Definition: inner product

An inner product is any rule \(\langle\vv{u}, \vv{v}\rangle\) that takes two "vectors" and returns a real number, such that

  • symmetric: \(\langle\vv{u}, \vv{v}\rangle = \langle\vv{v}, \vv{u}\rangle\);
  • linear in each slot: sums split and scalars pull out;
  • positive: \(\langle\vv{v}, \vv{v}\rangle > 0\) for every \(\vv{v}\) except zero.

The dot product is the standard example. Length is then defined as \(\sqrt{\langle\vv{v}, \vv{v}\rangle}\) and angle by the arccosine formula.

A small variation: the weighted dot product \(\langle\vv{u}, \vv{v}\rangle = \sum w_i u_i v_i\) with positive weights \(w_i\). It satisfies all three rules, and is what you want when the components have different importance or different units.

Functions as vectors

A bigger leap. A vector with \(n\) components can be seen as a function: give it an index \(i\), get back the number \(v_i\). A function \(f(x)\) on an interval is the same kind of object with a continuum of indices: give it a position \(x\), get back a number. It is a vector with infinitely many components.

What should its dot product be? Sample two functions \(f\) and \(g\) at many evenly spaced positions, turning each into a long list, and take the ordinary dot product of the lists. Multiply by the spacing between samples so that the answer does not blow up as the samples get denser. As the spacing shrinks toward zero, that sum settles to a limit, which calculus calls the integral of the product and which you can read as the signed area under the curve \(f(x)\,g(x)\):

\[ \langle f, g\rangle = \int_a^b f(x)\,g(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \sum_{i} f(x_i)\,g(x_i)\,\Delta x. \]

It is the receipt recipe once more: multiply matching entries and add, with "matching" now meaning "at the same position \(x\)". If integrals are unfamiliar, the right-hand side is all you need: it is what a computer does anyway.

With that in place, everything carries over. Two functions are orthogonal when \(\langle f, g\rangle = 0\). A function has a length. And one function can be projected onto another, using the very same formula as in section 10.

A glimpse of Fourier series

Take the interval from \(0\) to \(2\pi\) and the waves \(\sin x, \sin 2x, \sin 3x, \dots\) (one, two, three full wiggles). A remarkable fact: these are all orthogonal to one another under the function inner product. The product of two different ones has exactly as much area above the axis as below.

That makes them behave like perpendicular axes in function space, and so we can ask of any function, "how much of you lies along \(\sin 3x\)?" The answer is a projection coefficient, the same ratio of dot products as before:

\[ \text{amount of } \sin kx \text{ in } f \;=\; \frac{\langle f, \sin kx\rangle}{\langle \sin kx, \sin kx\rangle}. \]

Try it on a square wave, a function that is \(+1\) on the first half of the interval and \(-1\) on the second, which looks nothing like a smooth sine.

import math
from collections.abc import Callable

type Signal = Callable[[float], float]

SAMPLE_COUNT = 100_000
PERIOD = 2 * math.pi


def inner_product(left: Signal, right: Signal) -> float:
    """Dot product of two functions on [0, 2 pi): sample both, multiply, add, scale by the step."""
    step = PERIOD / SAMPLE_COUNT
    positions = [(sample_index + 0.5) * step for sample_index in range(SAMPLE_COUNT)]
    return sum(left(position) * right(position) for position in positions) * step


def sine_wave(frequency: int) -> Signal:
    return lambda position: math.sin(frequency * position)


def square_wave(position: float) -> float:
    return 1.0 if math.sin(position) >= 0 else -1.0


print(f"<sin 1x, sin 2x> = {inner_product(sine_wave(1), sine_wave(2)):+.4f}")
print(f"<sin 2x, sin 5x> = {inner_product(sine_wave(2), sine_wave(5)):+.4f}")
print(f"<sin 3x, sin 3x> = {inner_product(sine_wave(3), sine_wave(3)):+.4f}   (pi = {math.pi:.4f})")
print()
for frequency in range(1, 8):
    coefficient = inner_product(square_wave, sine_wave(frequency)) / inner_product(sine_wave(frequency), sine_wave(frequency))
    print(f"amount of sin {frequency}x in the square wave: {round(coefficient, 4) + 0.0:+.4f}")
print(f"4 / pi = {4 / math.pi:.4f}, 4 / (3 pi) = {4 / (3 * math.pi):.4f}")

The cross product is a different thing

In 3D there is a second famous product of vectors, the cross product \(\va\times\vb\). The two are often taught together and easy to mix up, so here they are side by side.

One last variation, for completeness. For vectors of complex numbers, the plain sum \(\sum a_ib_i\) fails the positivity rule (\(i \cdot i = -1\)), so one of the two vectors is conjugated: \(\langle\va, \vb\rangle = \sum a_i\overline{b_i}\). That version is the foundation of quantum mechanics, where the inner product of two states gives the probability amplitude of finding one in the other.

Problem 15.1 medium

Is \(\langle\va, \vb\rangle = a_1b_1 - a_2b_2\) an inner product on the plane? Check the three rules.

Solution

Symmetric: yes, swapping \(a\) and \(b\) changes nothing. Linear in each slot: yes, it is a sum of products of one \(a\)-component with one \(b\)-component. Positive: no. For \(\vv{v} = (0, 1)\), \(\langle\vv{v}, \vv{v}\rangle = -1\), and for \(\vv{v} = (1, 1)\) it is 0 although \(\vv{v}\) is not zero. So it is not an inner product: "length" would be the square root of a negative number, and the proof of Cauchy–Schwarz, which starts from \(0 \le \vv{r}\cdot\vv{r}\), collapses.

(This rule is not useless. With time as the minus-sign coordinate it is the geometry of special relativity. But it is a different geometry, without ordinary lengths and angles.)

Problem 15.2 medium

On the interval from \(-1\) to \(1\), with \(\langle f, g\rangle\) the area under \(f(x)g(x)\), argue without any calculus that the functions \(f(x) = 1\) and \(g(x) = x\) are orthogonal.

Hint

Sketch the product \(f(x)\,g(x)\) and look at the areas on each side of zero.

Solution

The product is \(1\cdot x = x\), a straight line through the origin. From \(0\) to \(1\) it encloses a triangle above the axis with base 1 and height 1, area \(\tfrac12\). From \(-1\) to \(0\) it encloses a congruent triangle below the axis, signed area \(-\tfrac12\). The total is 0, so \(\langle 1, x\rangle = 0\).

In the sampled picture: each sample at position \(x\) is cancelled by the one at \(-x\). The same symmetry argument shows that any even function is orthogonal to any odd function on a symmetric interval.

16Pitfalls

  • Returning a vector. The component-wise product \((a_1b_1, a_2b_2)\) is not the dot product; its sum is. NumPy makes this slip easy: a * b is component-wise, a @ b is the dot product.
  • Zero does not mean zero vector. \(\va\cdot\vb = 0\) says the vectors are perpendicular (or one is zero). With ordinary numbers a zero product forces a zero factor; with vectors it does not.
  • Forgetting to normalise. \(\va\cdot\vb\) equals \(\cos\th\) only when both are unit vectors. Lighting code with an unnormalised normal produces surfaces that are too bright or too dark, usually after the model has been scaled.
  • Comparing cosines the wrong way round. Cosine decreases as the angle grows. "Angle less than \(30^\circ\)" is "cosine greater than \(\cos 30^\circ\)".
  • Degrees into a radians function. math.cos(60) is the cosine of 60 radians, about \(-0.95\). Convert with math.radians.
  • Arccosine of 1.0000000000000002. Clamp the cosine into \([-1, 1]\) before calling acos. Better: if you only need a comparison, do not call acos at all.
  • The zero vector. It has no direction, so the angle and the cosine similarity are undefined (the formula divides by zero). Decide what your code should do with an empty document or a stationary object before it happens in production.
  • Dividing by the length once when projecting. The projection vector divides by \(\vb\cdot\vb\), the length squared: once to turn the dot product into a shadow length, once to turn \(\vb\) into a unit direction.
  • Reading a raw dot product as similarity. A large dot product can mean "aligned" or just "long". Cosine similarity ignores length; raw dot product rewards it. Which you want depends on whether length carries meaning in your data.
  • Different lengths. The dot product needs both lists to have the same number of entries. Python's zip silently truncates to the shorter one and returns a wrong answer; pass strict=True, as most of the code on this page does, or compare the lengths yourself.
  • Treating it as associative. \((\va\cdot\vb)\,\vv{c} \ne \va\,(\vb\cdot\vv{c})\) in general, and \(\va\cdot\vb\cdot\vv{c}\) means nothing.

17Summary

\[ \va\cdot\vb \;=\; \sum_i \ca{a_i}\cb{b_i} \;=\; \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\th \]Two formulas, one number. The left one is how you compute it; the right one is what it means.
\[ \va\cdot\vb \;=\; \cp{(\text{shadow of } \va \text{ on } \vb)} \times \norm{\vb} \]The picture to keep. Positive: same general direction. Zero: perpendicular. Negative: opposing.
\[ \norm{\va} = \sqrt{\va\cdot\va}, \qquad \cos\th = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}}, \qquad \cp{\operatorname{proj}_{\vb}\va} = \frac{\va\cdot\vb}{\vb\cdot\vb}\,\vb \]Length, angle and projection are all built from dot products alone.
\[ \va\cdot(\vv{u} + \vv{v}) = \va\cdot\vv{u} + \va\cdot\vv{v}, \qquad (k\va)\cdot\vb = k\,(\va\cdot\vb), \qquad \va\cdot\vb = \vb\cdot\va \]Brackets expand as in school algebra. This linearity is why the two formulas agree: shadows add, so you can work one axis at a time.
\[ |\va\cdot\vb| \le \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb} \]Cauchy–Schwarz: a shadow is never longer than what casts it. It is what makes "angle" meaningful in any dimension, and for any inner product, including one on functions.

Solution

\(\vv{u}\cdot\vv{v} = 1\cdot 1\cdot\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12\).

\(\norm{\vv{u} + \vv{v}}^2 = 1 + 2\cdot\tfrac12 + 1 = 3\), so \(\norm{\vv{u} + \vv{v}} = \sqrt3 \approx 1.732\).

\(\norm{\vv{u} - \vv{v}}^2 = 1 - 2\cdot\tfrac12 + 1 = 1\), so \(\norm{\vv{u} - \vv{v}} = 1\). That makes sense: two unit sides at \(60^\circ\) and a third side of length 1 form an equilateral triangle.

Q5 easy

Project \((4, 3)\) onto \((0, 2)\).

Solution

\(\dfrac{(4, 3)\cdot(0, 2)}{(0, 2)\cdot(0, 2)}\,(0, 2) = \dfrac{6}{4}\,(0, 2) = (0, 3)\). Projecting onto the y-axis keeps the y-component, as it should.

Q6 medium

Which of these expressions are meaningless, for vectors \(\va, \vb, \vv{c}\) in 3D? (i) \((\va\cdot\vb)\,\vv{c}\)   (ii) \(\va\cdot(\vb\cdot\vv{c})\)   (iii) \((\va\cdot\vb) + \vv{c}\)   (iv) \(\norm{\va}\,(\vb\cdot\vv{c})\)   (v) \((\va + \vb)\cdot\vv{c}\)

Solution

Track the type of each piece: a dot product is a number.

(i) number times vector: fine, a vector. (ii) vector dotted with a number: meaningless. (iii) number plus vector: meaningless. (iv) number times number: fine. (v) vector dot vector: fine.

Q7 medium, find the bug

This function should return True when the target is within max_angle_degrees of the forward direction. It returns the opposite. Why?

def is_facing(forward: list[float], to_target: list[float], max_angle_degrees: float) -> bool:
    cosine = dot(forward, to_target) / (length(forward) * length(to_target))
    return cosine <= math.cos(math.radians(max_angle_degrees))
Solution

The comparison is backwards. Cosine is a decreasing function from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ.\) So "angle at most the limit" is "cosine at least the cosine of the limit". The last line should use >=. Everything else, including the conversion to radians, is right.

Q8 medium

A matrix \(M\) has rows \(\vv{r}_1\) and \(\vv{r}_2\), and a vector \(\vv{x}\) satisfies \(M\vv{x} = \vv{0}\). What does that say about \(\vv{x}\) geometrically?

Solution

Each entry of \(M\vv{x}\) is a row dotted with \(\vv{x}\). So \(\vv{r}_1\cdot\vv{x} = 0\) and \(\vv{r}_2\cdot\vv{x} = 0\): \(\vv{x}\) is perpendicular to every row of \(M\). Solving \(M\vv{x} = \vv{0}\) means finding everything perpendicular to all the rows. This is the first step toward one of the central pictures of linear algebra (the null space is perpendicular to the row space).

Q9 medium

In 100 dimensions, let \(\va = (1, 1, \dots, 1)\) and \(\vb = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\). What is the angle between them? What happens to it as the dimension grows?

Solution

\(\va\cdot\vb = 1\), \(\norm{\va} = \sqrt{100} = 10\), \(\norm{\vb} = 1\). So \(\cos\th = 0.1\) and \(\th = \arccos(0.1) \approx 84.26^\circ\). In \(n\) dimensions \(\cos\th = 1/\sqrt n\), which tends to 0, so the angle tends to \(90^\circ\). The "main diagonal" becomes nearly perpendicular to every axis. Compare Problem 9.2, where \(n = 3\) gave \(54.74^\circ\).

Q10 hard

You rotate both \(\va\) and \(\vb\) by the same angle about the origin. All four components change. Explain, without computing, why \(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2\) comes out the same as before.

Solution

Because \(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 = \norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\th\), and a rotation changes neither lengths nor the angle between the two arrows. The right side depends only on the arrows as geometric objects, so the left side must too, however its individual terms shift around. This is the sense in which the dot product is "coordinate-free", and it is the deep reason the component formula is the right multiply-and-add rule and not an arbitrary one.

20History

The dot product is younger than calculus by two centuries, and it arrived wrapped inside something else.

The law of cosines is the oldest ingredient. Propositions 12 and 13 of Book II of Euclid's Elements (around 300 BC) state it in the language of areas, with no notion of cosine. The Persian mathematician Jamshīd al-Kāshī gave it in a trigonometric form suited to computation in the fifteenth century, and in French it is still called the théorème d'Al-Kashi.

Lambert's cosine law for the brightness of a tilted surface comes from Johann Heinrich Lambert's Photometria of 1760, long before anyone would have written it as a product of two vectors.

The product itself surfaced in the 1840s, twice. In 1843 William Rowan Hamilton discovered quaternions, four-component numbers whose multiplication mixes what we now separate: multiply two quaternions with no "real" part and the result's real part is minus the dot product of the two 3D vectors, while the remaining three components are their cross product. In 1844 Hermann Grassmann published his Ausdehnungslehre, a far-sighted and for decades largely unread theory of "extended quantities" in any number of dimensions, which included an inner product.

The modern form is due to Josiah Willard Gibbs at Yale and, independently, Oliver Heaviside in England, both in the 1880s and both motivated by Maxwell's electromagnetism. They pulled the two products out of the quaternion and treated them as separate operations on vectors. Gibbs circulated privately printed lecture notes, Elements of Vector Analysis (1881–84), in which he called them the "direct product" and the "skew product" and wrote them with a dot and a cross. His student Edwin Bidwell Wilson turned the lectures into the textbook Vector Analysis (1901), which fixed the notation for the following century; the everyday names "dot product" and "cross product" come from those symbols. The change did not go unopposed: supporters of quaternions and of vectors fought a long and sometimes bitter argument in the pages of Nature during the 1890s.

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality has three parents. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the version for finite sums in 1821. Viktor Bunyakovsky extended it to integrals in 1859. Hermann Schwarz rediscovered the integral form in the 1880s with the proof that is now standard, and in Russian-language texts the inequality carries all three names.

The latest chapter is the one from section 13. The 2017 paper that introduced the transformer named its central operation scaled dot-product attention.
